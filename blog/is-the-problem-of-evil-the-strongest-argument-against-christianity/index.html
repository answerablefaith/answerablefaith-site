Is the Problem of Evil the Strongest Argument Against Christianity?

The problem of evil is serious, but Christianity answers more than the claim that free will explains everything.

Published 2 September 2026 · Updated 2 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

The problem of evil is one of Christianity’s hardest objections, but it is not a logical disproof of God. Christianity distinguishes moral evil from natural evil, treats human rebellion as real, affirms God’s sovereign purposes without calling evil good, and centres its answer on the cross, resurrection, final judgment and new creation.

The problem of evil has force because evil is not abstract. People suffer abuse, war, disease, bereavement and disaster. A Christian answer should not begin by pretending this is easy. It should begin by asking what the argument actually proves, and whether Christianity already has categories for a world where God is good and powerful while evil is real.

Does evil logically rule out an all-good, all-powerful God?

Not by itself. The argument only works if an all-good God must prevent every evil immediately. Christianity denies that assumption. Scripture presents God as able to permit evil for a time without approving it, losing control of it or becoming its author.

Genesis 50:20 captures the distinction: Joseph’s brothers meant evil, while God meant the same event for good. The human intention remained wicked. God’s providence did not turn evil into goodness; it overruled evil toward a righteous end.

Does free will explain all suffering?

No, and Christians should not pretend it does. Human freedom helps explain moral evil: murder, betrayal, cruelty and oppression arise from creatures acting against God and neighbour. But earthquakes, cancer and other forms of natural suffering require a wider biblical frame.

Romans 8:18–25 describes creation itself as groaning and awaiting renewal. Christianity says the world is not presently in its final intended state. Human sin, death and disorder belong to a fallen creation that God will one day restore.

Why would God permit evil at all?

Christians do not claim to know God’s reason for every instance of suffering. That would be presumptuous. What Christianity does claim is that God can have morally sufficient reasons for permitting what He hates, and that His wisdom exceeds ours.

Reformed theology is especially clear that providence is not divine helplessness. God remains sovereign while creatures remain responsible. Acts 2:23 says Jesus was delivered up according to God’s definite plan, while those who crucified Him were still guilty. The cross itself therefore shows divine purpose and human evil operating together without collapsing into the same thing.

What about natural evil and children’s suffering?

This is where glib answers become pastorally damaging. We should not tell grieving people that a particular tragedy happened because they lacked faith or because God wanted to teach them a simple lesson. Christianity gives us permission to lament, protest and weep.

At the same time, suffering does not show that God is indifferent. In Christ, God enters the world’s suffering rather than observing it from safety. 1 Peter 3:18 says Christ suffered for sins, and the resurrection declares that death and evil will not have the final word.

Why is the cross central to the answer?

Because Christianity’s answer to evil is not merely philosophical. At the cross, God judges sin and bears suffering in the person of the Son. The worst human injustice in history becomes the means of redemption without ceasing to be injustice.

That matters because it shows God is neither morally detached from evil nor defeated by it. He can permit what He opposes, judge it rightly and bring good through it without becoming evil Himself.

Does Christianity promise that every evil will finally be answered?

Yes. Revelation 21:1–5 points to a renewed creation in which death, mourning and pain are removed. Final judgment means no hidden cruelty is forgotten, and new creation means suffering is not permanent.

In my view, this is where Christianity is strongest. It does not ask us to rename evil as good. It says evil is genuinely evil, God is genuinely sovereign, the cross is genuinely costly, and restoration will be genuinely final.

How to say it gently

You could say: “The problem of evil is one of Christianity’s hardest questions, and free will alone does not answer every form of suffering. Christians believe God can permit evil without approving it, that creation itself is fallen, and that God enters suffering in Christ, judges evil and promises final restoration.”

What not to say

Do not say, “Everything happens for a reason,” as though that explains a person’s pain. Do not make free will carry the whole argument when natural suffering is part of the objection. And do not speak as though we know God’s specific reason for every tragedy. Christian confidence should be strong where Scripture is strong and humble where Scripture is not specific.
