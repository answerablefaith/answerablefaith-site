Why Should I Forgive Someone Who Hurt Me?

Christian forgiveness refuses revenge without denying harm, removing consequences or demanding unsafe reconciliation.

Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Practise in the app Back to blog
Answer in brief

Christians forgive because God has forgiven them in Christ, and because personal revenge must not rule their lives. Forgiveness does not excuse harm, erase consequences or require renewed contact. Trust must be earned, and reconciliation needs truth and repentance. If someone is unsafe, seeking protection and justice is right.

When someone hurts you, forgiveness may feel unfair. You may fear it means saying the harm no longer matters. The Bible does call Christians to forgive, but it never asks us to call evil good. The first step is to understand what forgiveness is, and what it is not.

Why does Jesus command forgiveness?

Jesus teaches His followers to forgive because they have received mercy themselves. Matthew 6:12–15 joins our plea for God's forgiveness to our duty to forgive others. Ephesians 4:31–32 grounds that duty in the grace God has given us in Christ.

This is a serious call, not a claim that every offence is small. The cross shows God's mercy without pretending that sin has no cost. See why forgiveness and judgment belong together.

Does forgiveness mean saying what happened was acceptable?

No. Forgiveness begins by naming a real wrong. You cannot meaningfully forgive what you insist was harmless. A person may need to speak honestly about betrayal, theft, violence or abuse before deciding how to respond.

In Romans 12:17–21, Paul rejects personal vengeance while calling evil what it is. Christians are to seek peace where possible and leave final judgment to God. That differs from protecting an offender from consequences or pretending that anger and grief disappear on command.

The stronger distinction is between refusing revenge and refusing justice. Christian forgiveness calls us away from revenge; it does not call us away from justice.

Must I trust or reconcile with someone I have forgiven?

No. Forgiveness, trust and reconciliation are different. Forgiveness concerns how you respond to a wrong and the person who committed it. Trust concerns whether that person is safe and reliable. Reconciliation is the repair of a damaged relationship, which cannot be achieved by one person alone.

Jesus speaks about repentance in Luke 17:3–4. Repentance is more than a quick apology. It should show itself in changed conduct over time. Sometimes trust can be rebuilt; sometimes the danger remains. Forgiveness does not guarantee restored access.

Boston College's guide to forgiveness and reconciliation also distinguishes personal forgiveness from the slower work of restoring trust.

What if the person who hurt me is abusive or dangerous?

Your safety matters. If someone is threatening, violent or controlling, you can set boundaries, leave, seek help and report crimes. None of those actions is a failure to forgive. Church leaders must never use a Bible verse to send someone back into danger.

Our guide on responding to church scandals explains why protecting victims and telling the truth matter more than defending an institution. A Presbyterian Church in America study on domestic abuse warns against unsafe confrontation and says reconciliation requires sustained evidence of repentance.

Where there is immediate danger, contact emergency services. Where abuse has occurred, seek support from trustworthy people and qualified safeguarding or trauma-informed professionals. You do not owe an offender a private meeting to prove your faith.

What if I still feel angry after choosing to forgive?

Pain does not switch off when you make a decision. Memories, fear and anger may return, especially after serious harm. Forgiveness may involve repeated prayer and patient help rather than one dramatic moment.

Psalm 13 gives words for grief and distress. Nor should anyone use Matthew 6:14–15 to threaten a traumatised person into immediate contact with an offender. Jesus' warning against a hard, vengeful heart is serious; so is His concern for the vulnerable.

It may help to ask: “What would it mean to refuse revenge today while still seeking safety and truth?” Christian care for justice is explored further in why Christians must care about justice.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Jesus asks us to forgive because He has shown us mercy. But that does not mean what happened was acceptable. You can seek justice, stay safe and keep boundaries. I would not ask you to trust someone who has not shown change. May I listen to what you have been through?”

What not to say

Do not tell a victim that forgiveness means forgetting, withdrawing a report or returning to an unsafe home. Do not set a deadline for recovery. Avoid using “forgive and move on” as a way to stop listening. It is wrong to suggest a person must reconcile before God will care for them.
