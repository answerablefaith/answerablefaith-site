What Does “Begotten, Not Made” Mean About Jesus?

“Begotten, not made” does not mean Jesus came into existence; it confesses that the Son is eternally from the Father, fully divine and never part of creation.

Published 11 September 2026 · Updated 11 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

“Begotten, not made” does not mean the Son was created or began to exist. In Nicene Christianity, “begotten” names the Son’s eternal relation to the Father: the Son is from the Father yet fully shares the one divine nature. He is eternally Son, not a creature who later became divine.

The Nicene Creed uses “begotten” to distinguish the Son from everything God created. The phrase is about who the Son eternally is, not an event in which He first came to be. That is why the Creed immediately adds “not made” and confesses the Son as fully God.

Does “begotten” mean Jesus had a beginning?

No. Human begetting happens in time, so the word can sound as though the Son began after the Father. The Creed denies that idea. It speaks of the Son as “begotten of the Father before all worlds” and contrasts Him with things that are made. The doctrine is usually called the eternal generation of the Son.

What is often missed is that two questions are being confused. One is whether the Son is eternally divine. Scripture answers that clearly. The other is how the Son is personally related to the Father within the one God. Eternal generation is the classical Christian way of expressing that relation without making the Son a creature or a second God.

Why does the Creed say “begotten, not made”?

Because “from the Father” and “created by the Father” are not the same claim. Created things receive existence from nothing and belong to creation. The Son does not. John 1:1–3 says the Word already was with God and was God, and that all created things came into being through Him. If all created things were made through the Word, the Word Himself is not one of the created things.

The Creed therefore says “God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made”. Its aim is not to describe a biological process in God. It protects two biblical truths at once: the Son is genuinely distinct from the Father, and the Son fully possesses the divine nature. Our article on whether the Trinity is a contradiction explains why personal distinction does not mean three gods.

Does the Bible teach that the Son is eternal?

Yes. The Son’s eternal deity does not depend on one disputed translation of one word. John 17:5 records Jesus speaking of the glory He had with the Father before the world existed. Colossians 1:15–17 says all things were created through and for Christ and that He is before all things. Hebrews 1:1–3 calls the Son the radiance of God’s glory and the exact imprint of His nature.

That is why “Son of God” should not be heard as though God produced a lesser divine being. See our article on what “Son of God” means. Likewise, Paul’s description of Christ as “firstborn” does not place Him inside creation; the “firstborn” language in Colossians 1 speaks of His supremacy over it.

Does “only begotten” prove eternal generation by itself?

No single English phrase should carry the whole doctrine. The Greek word monogenēs, used of the Son in John, can stress uniqueness or being one-of-a-kind. Christians therefore should not argue that the English expression “only begotten” settles every exegetical question by itself.

That concession does not make the Nicene doctrine collapse. The stronger case is cumulative: Scripture distinguishes Father and Son, presents the Son as eternally divine and places Him on the Creator side of the Creator–creature distinction. Classical theology then uses “eternal generation” to name the Son’s timeless relation to the Father. The Nicene Creed as published by the Christian Reformed Church preserves that confession, and the Westminster Confession of Faith likewise describes the Son as eternally begotten of the Father.

Why does eternal generation matter?

It matters because Christian doctrine must distinguish the persons without dividing the divine being. If Father and Son differ only because of what happens in history, we still need to explain their eternal personal distinction. If the Son is made, His full deity is lost. Eternal generation says neither: the Father is eternally Father, the Son is eternally Son, and both wholly share the one divine nature.

There is real debate among Christians over which biblical texts most directly ground the doctrine and how terms such as monogenēs should be translated. That debate should be stated honestly. But the Nicene claim itself is deliberately anti-creationist: “begotten” is paired with “not made” precisely to deny that the Son began to exist.

How to say it gently

You could say: “When the Creed calls Jesus ‘begotten’, it is not saying He was created at some point. It is describing His eternal relation to the Father. The same Creed immediately says ‘not made’, because the Son is fully God and stands with the Father on the Creator side of the Creator–creature distinction.”

What not to say

Do not compare the Father and Son to human reproduction as though God produced another being. Do not pretend the word monogenēs by itself settles the whole doctrine. And do not dismiss Christians who ask how eternal generation is grounded in Scripture. The better answer is to show both the biblical case for the Son’s eternal deity and what the Nicene language is trying to safeguard.
