Who Made God?

The question sounds simple, but it helps us see the difference between the Creator and everything He made.

Published 27 July 2026 · Updated 27 July 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

No one made God. Everything that begins to exist needs a cause, but God never began to exist. Christianity describes God as eternal, uncreated and self-existent. If God had been made by something else, that maker would be greater and would be the true God instead.

Christians do not believe God is one more object inside the universe. God is the reason there is a universe at all. So asking “Who made God?” can be useful, but it assumes God belongs to the same class as created things. He does not. The question becomes clearer once we distinguish between beings that receive existence and the One whose existence is not borrowed from anything else.

Does everything need a cause?

No. The Christian claim is more precise: everything that begins to exist needs a cause. A chair, a star and a human life all begin. God does not. Psalm 90:1–2 says, “From everlasting to everlasting you are God.” God has no first moment and therefore does not require a prior maker.

This is why the answer is not a special exception invented to protect belief. An eternal God is part of what Christians mean by God. Exodus 3:13–15 records the divine name, “I AM WHO I AM.” God does not receive existence from another. He simply is.

Our view is that this distinction is the hinge of the whole discussion. Many objections sound decisive only because they treat God as a very powerful creature. Historic Christianity does not. It speaks of God as the uncreated source of every created reality.

Why not say the universe is eternal instead?

That is a fair question, but it moves the discussion rather than ending it. The universe is made of changing, dependent things. Its parts do not explain why the whole exists. Even an endless chain of dependent causes would still leave us asking why that chain exists at all.

Christianity points to a necessary Creator who does not depend on anything else. Romans 11:33–36 says, “From him and through him and to him are all things.” God is not the last link in a chain. He is the source of the whole chain.

This way of reasoning is not unique to modern apologetics. Classical Christian theology has long distinguished between contingent things, which may or may not exist, and God, whose existence is not dependent. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s overview of cosmological arguments explains how this family of arguments asks why contingent reality exists at all rather than assuming that every being has a cause.

We would add that saying “the universe just exists” does not offer a deeper explanation than saying “God just exists”. The question is which candidate can actually carry the explanatory weight. A changing, composite and dependent universe does not appear to possess existence in itself, whereas the Christian doctrine of God specifically identifies Him as self-existent and necessary.

Is this just avoiding the question?

It would be avoidance to say, “Everything needs a maker, except God.” But that is not the argument. The claim is that created and changing things need an explanation beyond themselves. God is eternal and uncreated, so He is not the kind of being that needs to be made.

Scripture repeatedly presents this Creator-creature distinction. Isaiah 40:25–28 describes the Lord as everlasting and as the Creator of the ends of the earth. John 1:1–3 says that all created things came into being through the eternal Word. The biblical picture is consistent: God is not part of the created order He explains.

We should also admit that eternity is hard to picture. Our minds are used to beginnings, clocks and causes. Not being able to imagine eternal existence fully does not make it contradictory. It shows the limits of creatures thinking about their Creator. The same humility is useful when discussing whether the Trinity is a contradiction: mystery is not the same as incoherence.

Does an eternal God solve every question?

No single argument answers every question about God. The claim that God is uncreated explains why asking for God’s maker misunderstands the Christian doctrine of God, but further questions remain: Why believe this God is personal? Why identify Him with the God revealed in Scripture? How does creation relate to divine freedom?

Those are real questions, and Christians should not pretend otherwise. Yet it is still important to answer one question at a time. The issue here is whether an uncreated Creator is conceptually coherent. It is. The next stage is to ask what evidence points towards that Creator and whether the Christian account best explains reality. Readers wrestling with God’s apparent absence may also find value in our discussion of the divine hiddenness argument.

Our conclusion is that “Who made God?” is best treated as the beginning of a deeper conversation, not as a trap to escape. It invites us to ask what kind of explanation could finally account for everything else without itself being dependent on something prior.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Christians do not believe everything has a cause. We believe everything that begins to exist has a cause. God did not begin. If He had a maker, that maker would be God. The Christian claim is that the eternal God is the source of everything else.”

What not to say

Do not mock the question or call it foolish. Many people ask it sincerely, and some Christians have asked it too. Avoid pretending eternity is easy to understand. Give the clear answer, then ask what the person thinks could finally explain why anything exists. Where you are unsure how to continue, use the principles in what to do when you do not know the answer: be honest, check the evidence and keep the conversation gracious.

A useful next step is to read Psalm 90:1–2, Exodus 3:13–15 and Acts 17:24–25 together. Notice that the Bible does not begin by explaining where God came from. It presents Him as the eternal, self-sufficient Creator from whom all life comes. That foundation also helps Christians answer difficult questions without becoming defensive.
