Does Genesis Contradict Modern Science?

Genesis and modern science ask overlapping but not identical questions, and Christians should not confuse one disputed reading of Genesis with Christianity itself.

Published 18 September 2026 · Updated 18 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Genesis does not require Christians to treat Scripture as a modern science textbook. It clearly teaches that God created the world, creation is good, humans bear God’s image and sin is real. Christians disagree about the age of the earth and evolution, so those disputed interpretations should not be confused with the core claims of Genesis.

Genesis does make claims about the real world, so Christians should not solve the question by saying science and faith never overlap. But the Bible’s opening chapters are theological revelation, not a modern laboratory report. The key task is to distinguish what Genesis clearly teaches from conclusions Christians infer about scientific mechanisms and chronology.

What does Genesis clearly teach?

Genesis 1 is unambiguous about the Creator: the heavens and the earth exist because God made them. Creation is ordered and repeatedly called good. Human beings are uniquely made in God’s image in Genesis 1:26–28, and Genesis 3 presents human rebellion as a real rupture in God’s good creation.

Those claims matter more to the passage than whether a reader can derive a geological timescale from the word “day”. Genesis tells us who made the world, what kind of world it is, what humans are for and why creation is now disordered by sin.

Does believing the earth is old mean rejecting the Bible?

No. Christians who affirm biblical authority have held different views about the length and structure of the creation days. Some read them as ordinary days and defend a young earth. Others understand the days as a literary framework, analogical days or long periods, while still affirming that Genesis is true Scripture.

The stronger question is therefore not, “Do you take the Bible seriously?” but, “What kind of text is Genesis 1, and what is it claiming?” Our article on why God reveals Himself through Scripture explains why genre and authorial purpose matter when we interpret a text faithfully.

Historic Reformed theology also leaves room for care here. The Westminster Confession of Faith strongly affirms God as Creator without turning its doctrine of creation into a modern scientific model.

Can a Christian accept evolution?

Orthodox Christians disagree over how evolutionary theory relates to Adam, human origins and the interpretation of Genesis. That disagreement should be admitted rather than hidden. What cannot simply be surrendered is the biblical claim that humanity is God’s creation, bears His image and stands in a real history of sin and redemption.

This means “evolution” is too broad a word to settle the theology by itself. A biological account of common descent is one question. A materialist claim that humans are nothing but accidental products of purposeless forces is another. Science can describe natural processes; it does not, by that description alone, establish that God does not create through providence.

That distinction is often missed in current debate. Rejecting philosophical naturalism does not require rejecting every scientific account of natural history. And accepting a scientific model does not require treating Genesis as disposable.

Is Genesis trying to answer modern scientific questions?

Not in the form modern readers usually ask them. Genesis arose in an ancient setting and directly challenges rival views in which the sun, moon, sea or other parts of nature were divine. Its opening chapter strips creation of divinity: the lights are made by God, the seas are ruled by God, and humans are created to image God rather than serve competing deities.

That does not make Genesis less factual. It means factual writing can have a theological purpose. We make a similar distinction when reading poetry, parable, history or prophecy elsewhere in Scripture. Treating every biblical sentence as though it were written in the genre of a scientific journal is not a higher view of Scripture; it can be a failure to read Scripture on its own terms.

What if science and my reading of Genesis seem to conflict?

Then Christians should examine both the science and the interpretation. Scientific models are open to testing and revision. Human interpretations of Scripture can also be mistaken. The existence of tension does not give us permission to dismiss Scripture, but neither should we assume our first reading is identical with the text itself.

In my view, this is where the discussion becomes more useful. The question is not whether “the Bible” and “science” are enemies in the abstract. It is which scientific claim is being made, which biblical claim is being made, and whether they actually contradict one another in the same respect.

That same habit helps with broader questions about evidence and extraordinary claims and how Christians compare competing truth claims.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Christians believe Genesis is true, but Christians do not all agree that Genesis gives us a modern scientific timeline. It clearly says God created the world, humans bear His image and sin is real. The next question is which parts of our scientific or biblical interpretation are actually in conflict.”

What not to say

Do not say that science has nothing to do with faith, because Scripture makes claims about the real world. Do not say every Christian must hold one age-of-the-earth position to be orthodox. And do not dismiss people as anti-science or anti-Bible before clarifying what they actually believe.
