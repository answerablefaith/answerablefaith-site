If God Cannot Change, How Can He Answer Prayer?

Prayer does not make God become a different God; it is one of the real means the unchanging God has ordained to accomplish His purposes.

Published 26 August 2026 · Updated 26 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

God can answer prayer without changing His nature, character or eternal purpose. Prayer really changes circumstances because God has ordained prayer as one of the means by which He acts. From our side, we ask and receive. From God’s side, He eternally knows and wills both the prayer and the answer.

If God already knows what He will do, why pray? And if God is unchanging, does an answered prayer mean we changed His mind? These are serious questions because bad answers make prayer sound either pointless or manipulative. Historic Christian theology offers a third option: God does not change in who He is, yet He truly acts in history and responds to His people.

What does it mean that God does not change?

Malachi 3:6 says, “I the LORD do not change.” Christians have understood this to mean that God does not become morally better, lose knowledge, revise His character or drift from one purpose to another because He discovered something new.

Immutability does not mean God is frozen or inactive. An unchanging God can create, judge, save, comfort and answer prayer while remaining perfectly faithful to His nature. The Westminster Confession describes God as “immutable” while also affirming His works of providence. Those claims belong together, not against each other.

If God already knows, why ask?

Jesus Himself says in Matthew 6:8 that the Father knows what we need before we ask Him. He then immediately teaches His disciples to pray. So Jesus does not treat divine foreknowledge as a reason to stop praying.

The reason is that prayer is not mainly information transfer. We are not updating God. Prayer is dependence, communion, worship and petition. More importantly, God can ordain an outcome together with the means that lead to it. If God purposes to give something through prayer, then the prayer is part of the purpose rather than an attempt to overturn it.

Can prayer really change what happens?

Yes, in the ordinary biblical sense. James 5:16–18 says the prayer of a righteous person has great power and points to Elijah’s prayers as genuinely effective. Scripture does not tell us to act as though outcomes are fixed in a way that makes asking meaningless.

Reformed theology often explains this through means and ends. God ordains not only what happens but also how it happens. He ordains food as a means of nourishment, preaching as a means of hearing the gospel, and prayer as a means through which He gives, withholds, comforts, redirects and acts.

Does that mean prayer changes God’s mind?

Not if by “changes God’s mind” we mean that God discovers new information, abandons one eternal plan and improvises another. Scripture often describes God responding to people in real time because that is how His relationship with creatures unfolds in history. From our perspective, there is a real before and after: we pray, and something happens that would not have happened apart from that prayer.

But from God’s perspective, the prayer itself was never a surprise. Isaiah 46:9–10 presents God as declaring the end from the beginning. His eternal purpose can include a real human request and a real divine answer without requiring God to become a different kind of being.

Then why are many prayers unanswered?

Because prayer is not a mechanism for controlling providence. The Father invites us to ask, but He remains wise and sovereign. Even Jesus prayed, “not as I will, but as you will” in Matthew 26:39. Christian prayer holds bold asking and trust together.

This is where some current discussions go wrong. One side can make prayer sound like persuading a reluctant God to revise His plan. The other can make sovereignty sound as if prayer changes nothing. Neither fits the biblical pattern. God is sovereign enough to ordain genuine prayer as a genuine cause within His providence.

How should Christians think about prayer practically?

Pray because God commands it, welcomes it and uses it. Ask specifically. Thank Him. Lament honestly. Seek wisdom. Pray for healing and provision while refusing to treat God as a machine. The fact that God already knows is not a reason for silence; it is a reason for confidence that the One hearing us is never confused or caught off guard.

In my view, this is one of the places where Reformed theology is pastorally stronger than it first sounds. Divine sovereignty does not make prayer smaller. It gives prayer a foundation. We are not throwing wishes into an uncertain universe. We are speaking to a Father whose purposes are stable enough to include our prayers without being threatened by them.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Christians do not think prayer gives God information He lacked or forces Him to change His character. We believe God has chosen to use prayer as one of the real means through which He carries out His purposes. He knows the prayer beforehand, but the prayer still genuinely matters.”

What not to say

Do not tell someone, “Prayer only changes you.” Scripture says prayer can affect circumstances. Do not say, “If you prayed with enough faith, God would have done it,” as though every unanswered prayer proves spiritual failure. And do not make sovereignty sound fatalistic. Biblical providence includes real human choices, requests and means.
