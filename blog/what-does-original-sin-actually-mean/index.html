What Does Original Sin Actually Mean?

Original sin explains humanity’s fallen nature and solidarity with Adam—and why our deepest need is the grace of Christ.

Published 9 October 2026 · Updated 9 October 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Original sin means humanity is fallen through Adam: we inherit a corrupt nature and are subject to sin and death. In classical Reformed teaching, Adam’s guilt is also counted to his descendants because he represented humanity. This does not mean each baby consciously committed Adam’s act. The Christian answer is Christ, the new Adam, who brings righteousness and life.

Original sin does not mean that newborn children knowingly choose evil. It means our problem with sin begins deeper than isolated bad choices. The Bible presents the human race as fallen in Adam, marked by death and an inward tendency to turn away from God. The doctrine is serious, but its purpose is to explain why we need grace rather than mere self-improvement.

What did humanity inherit from Adam?

Christians agree that Adam’s rebellion brought sin and death into human history. In Romans 5:12–19, Paul says that sin entered through one man and that many were made sinners through his disobedience. 1 Corinthians 15:21–22 connects death through Adam with resurrection through Christ.

Original sin has two related parts in classical Reformed theology. Corruption means our nature is disordered: we do not love God as we should. Imputed guilt means Adam acted as humanity’s representative, so his first transgression is counted to those he represented. The Westminster Confession of Faith, chapter 6 states both claims.

Other Christian traditions explain inherited guilt differently. Eastern Orthodox Christians often stress ancestral mortality and corruption rather than guilt imputed in the same way. That is a real theological disagreement, not a reason to hide what the Reformed tradition teaches.

Does original sin mean babies personally committed Adam’s choice?

No child personally stood in Eden and ate the forbidden fruit. Reformed teaching does not claim otherwise. Its claim about guilt is representative, not that each infant consciously chose Adam’s action. An analogy of shared consequences is useful only up to a point: Paul speaks of a deeper union with Adam than ordinary family influence.

The fairness objection deserves attention. Why should anyone be affected by another person’s act? Christianity replies that human beings are connected through representatives. Paul places Adam beside Christ: through the disobedience of one, condemnation comes; through the obedience of one, justification and life are offered. If we reject representation in principle, we must also ask what that would mean for Christ acting on behalf of sinners.

That answer explains the biblical structure; it does not make every moral difficulty vanish. We should not use the doctrine to speculate about the eternal fate of particular infants.

Does Ezekiel 18 contradict original sin?

No. Ezekiel 18 rebukes Israelites who blamed their fathers while refusing to repent. Its message is that each person is answerable for their own wrongdoing. Ezekiel 18:20 does not teach that the human race never shares a fallen condition or that Adam had no representative role.

In my view, the key is to separate personal acts from human solidarity. Ezekiel addresses whether someone may excuse their present sins by pointing to their parents. Romans 5 explains why sin and death mark the whole human family. Those are connected questions, but they are not identical.

Are people as evil as they could possibly be?

No. The doctrine of original sin is not the claim that every person always does the worst thing available. People show courage, affection, honesty and generosity. They remain made in God’s image. Yet Psalm 51:5 and Ephesians 2:1–5 describe sin as a condition reaching into our hearts and lives.

Calling us fallen does not mean denying genuine human good. It means that even our best acts do not restore us to perfect love of God. See our articles on why salvation is by grace and how faith and works belong together.

Why does original sin make Christ necessary?

If sin were only a collection of poor habits, advice and effort might be enough. Scripture says we need rescue, forgiveness and a new heart. In Romans 5, Paul’s answer to Adam is not a better version of Adam but Jesus Christ. Christ obeys where Adam failed, bears judgment for sinners and gives new life to those united to Him by faith.

That links original sin with why God’s forgiveness does not ignore sin. God does not dismiss the damage caused by human evil; He deals with sin through Christ and gives grace to people who cannot save themselves. The story begins in Genesis 3, but it does not end there. Our article on what Genesis teaches about creation and human rebellion explains why that account must be read for what it actually claims.

How to say it gently

You might say: “Christians do not mean that babies consciously sinned in Eden. We mean that humanity is born into a fallen condition through Adam. Christians debate exactly how inherited guilt works, but we agree the problem reaches deeper than bad examples. The Christian hope is that Christ brings a new beginning.”

What not to say

Do not say everyone is as wicked as possible, or that infants personally performed Adam’s action. Do not present the inherited-guilt debate as though all Christian traditions use the same language. And do not end with Adam: the doctrine is meant to point us towards the greater grace found in Christ.
