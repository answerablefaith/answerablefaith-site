What Does Original Sin Actually Mean?

Original sin describes our fallen condition in Adam and shows why we need the grace of Jesus Christ.

Published 9 October 2026 · Updated 9 October 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Original sin means we enter life sharing Adam’s fallen condition, not that infants consciously repeated his choice. Scripture teaches both inherited corruption and Adam’s representative guilt. Our own sins remain truly ours. Paul’s answer is Jesus Christ, whose obedience and saving work bring righteousness and new life to those united to Him by faith.

What does original sin actually mean? It does not mean newborn children knowingly choose evil. It means the human problem with sin is deeper than isolated bad decisions. We inherit a fallen nature and belong to a humanity whose first representative disobeyed God. The doctrine can feel severe, but its purpose is to explain why we need grace rather than better habits.

We think the first distinction is especially useful: being born sinful is not the same as personally committing Adam’s act. Confusing those claims makes the doctrine sound less careful than Scripture does.

What did humanity inherit from Adam?

Paul says sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin. In Romans 5:12–19, Adam’s disobedience affects the many he represents. 1 Corinthians 15:21–22 also connects death through Adam with resurrection through Christ. Original sin has two related parts: inherited corruption and representative guilt.

Corruption means our hearts are bent away from God. We can still love, think, create and act kindly because we remain made in His image. Yet no part of life is untouched by sin, and we cannot heal the problem by effort alone. Representative guilt means Adam’s first offence is counted to the humanity he represented. Paul does not treat Adam merely as a bad example whom everyone later copies.

These claims have a long history in Christian theology. Augustine discusses inherited sin in On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins. His account is not a substitute for Scripture, but it shows that Christians wrestled with the question centuries before present debates.

Does original sin mean babies personally committed Adam’s choice?

No infant stood in Eden and chose Adam’s action. Personal wrongdoing and representative guilt must not be confused. The claim is that Adam acted as the head of a human race that fell in him, not that each infant made a conscious decision in the garden.

That raises a fair objection: why should another person’s act count against us? Paul’s argument does not answer by pretending representation is easy to accept. Instead, he sets Adam beside Christ. One man’s disobedience brings condemnation; one man’s obedience is the basis for the justification of those united to Him. The comparison is central, not an afterthought.

We would not claim that every question about fairness vanishes once we use the word “representative”. Rather, Paul places human solidarity at the centre of both the problem and its remedy. If we reject anyone acting for others as inherently unjust, we also need to explain how Christ could act for us.

None of this permits us to pronounce the eternal destiny of a particular child. We should distinguish what Scripture teaches about the fallen condition from what it has not revealed about individual cases.

Does Ezekiel 18 contradict original sin?

Ezekiel 18 challenges people who blame their parents while refusing to answer for their own conduct. Verse 20 says the son will not bear the guilt of his father’s personal wrongdoing. The chapter calls hearers to repent rather than hide behind family history.

Romans 5 addresses a different question: why are sin and death universal, and how does the obedience of Christ answer Adam’s disobedience? Ezekiel is not discussing Adam’s unique role as humanity’s representative. Paul is not excusing anyone’s own sin. We must give each passage the question it is answering.

Our view is that this distinction also protects personal responsibility. Original sin does not mean “Adam made me do it”. Our choices are really ours, even though they come from hearts already affected by the fall.

Are people as evil as they could possibly be?

No. Original sin does not mean everyone always chooses the worst possible action. People can be generous, courageous and loving. Human beings remain God’s image-bearers, even after the fall. What the doctrine says is that sin reaches through the whole person, not that every action is equally wicked.

Psalm 51:5 speaks of sin from the beginning of life, while Ephesians 2:1–5 describes humanity’s need for God to make us alive by grace. The point is not to deny good things we see in people. It is to deny that those good things can remove guilt or give us the new heart we need.

Here we think the doctrine is unusually honest about human nature. We are capable of real kindness and real cruelty, sometimes in the same day. Calling us fallen explains why moral instruction helps but cannot, by itself, reconcile us to God. Our article on why grace produces good works develops that distinction.

Why does original sin make Christ necessary?

If sin were only a collection of bad habits, advice might be enough. Scripture says we need forgiveness, reconciliation and new life. In Romans 5, the answer to Adam is not a better moral example alone. It is Jesus Christ, whose obedience succeeds where Adam failed and whose saving work restores people who could never restore themselves.

Romans 5:18–21 does not teach that everyone automatically receives the saving benefits of Christ. Paul is contrasting two representative heads and their outcomes. Those united to Christ by faith receive a righteousness they did not earn. This is why justification by faith and the fruit of obedience belong together without confusing the two.

We think the symmetry between Adam and Christ is what gives the doctrine its hope. If our fall went deeper than our choices, our rescue must go deeper too. God does not merely offer a fresh set of rules. He gives us a new standing in Christ and works to renew the people He saves.

How to say it gently

You might say: “Original sin does not mean babies personally chose Adam’s sin. It means humanity is fallen through Adam, and sin reaches our hearts before we can fix ourselves. Christians believe Adam represented humanity, just as Christ represents those He saves. The good news is that grace reaches deeper than the problem.”

What not to say

Do not say everyone is as wicked as possible, or that infants knowingly committed Adam’s act. Do not dismiss the fairness objection as foolish. And do not stop with the fall: original sin is best understood in the same passage that announces the greater grace of Jesus Christ.
