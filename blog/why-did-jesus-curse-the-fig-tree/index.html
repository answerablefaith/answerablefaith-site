Why Did Jesus Curse the Fig Tree?

Jesus’ striking act in Mark 11 is a warning about fruitless worship, not a fit of anger at a tree.

Published 13 November 2026 · Updated 13 November 2026

Practise in the app Back to blog
Answer in brief

Jesus cursed the fig tree as a prophetic warning, not because a plant had wronged Him. Mark places the act around Jesus’ challenge to the temple’s fruitless worship. The detail that figs were out of season makes the scene harder, but it points us beyond botany to the warning Jesus chose to enact.

In Mark 11:12–25, Jesus is hungry, finds a leafy fig tree without fruit and says that no one will eat from it again. The tree then withers. It sounds harsh, especially because Mark says it was not the season for figs. The question deserves more than “Jesus can do what He likes.” Mark gives us clues about what Jesus was doing.

Why would Jesus curse a tree that had no figs?

The fig tree serves as a prophetic sign. Jesus is not treating a tree as a person who has sinned. Plants have no moral duty to bear fruit on demand. The act is meant to be seen and understood by His disciples.

The Hebrew prophets used the loss of fruit to picture the consequences of unfaithfulness. In Jeremiah 8:13, judgment is described through a fig tree without figs and leaves that fail. Jesus’ action belongs in that biblical pattern: a visible sign of a serious warning.

Why does Mark say it was not the season for figs?

Mark’s detail makes the objection stronger, not weaker. Jesus looked for figs when the ordinary harvest was not due. Some commentators suggest early edible growth might have been expected on a leafy tree. Others question whether that explains the text. We do not need to settle that botanical debate to read the story.

The key distinction is between a literal fault and a symbolic message. If a tree was out of season, it was not guilty of failing Jesus. The question is why Jesus chose this surprising act to teach His followers. Mark answers by placing another event between the curse and its result.

What does the fig tree have to do with the temple?

Mark tells the story in three stages: Jesus speaks to the tree, confronts abuses in the Jerusalem temple, and then the disciples find the tree withered. Those scenes interpret one another.

In Mark 11:15–19, Jesus objects to what the temple courts have become. He cites Isaiah 56:7, where God’s house is for prayer among all nations, and Jeremiah 7:11, a warning against religious life that masks wrongdoing. Busy worship can look healthy while failing to honour God.

The fig tree gives that warning a shape: leaves are visible, but fruit is missing. This temple connection is supported by Fuller Seminary’s study of the passage and a scholarly study of Mark’s narrative. The point is not that every religious practice is false, but that outward activity cannot stand in for faithfulness.

Was Jesus condemning all Jewish people?

No. Jesus, His disciples and the first Christians were Jewish. The story must not be used to blame Jewish people as a whole or to excuse antisemitism. The immediate challenge concerns the temple’s worship and leadership in Jesus’ own time. Interpreters debate the full scope of the judgment sign, so it is better to name that setting than make sweeping claims about a people.

The warning can still search Christian hearts today. As our article on responding to church scandals shows, religious status does not excuse harm or hypocrisy.

Why does Jesus speak about faith and prayer afterwards?

When Peter points out the withered tree, Jesus calls the disciples to trust God, pray and forgive (Mark 11:20–25). That is not an unrelated ending. A passage that challenges corrupt worship also points towards the trust and mercy that should mark genuine worship.

Jesus is not promising that any wish will come true if we believe hard enough. His wider teaching matters; see what Jesus meant by asking and receiving in prayer. Nor should we treat this act as permission to curse people who disappoint us. The one who judges hypocrisy also teaches His followers to forgive.

Is the prophetic reading the only Christian interpretation?

It is a strong reading because of Mark’s order of events and the Old Testament imagery, but scholars differ over details. Some stress judgment on the temple leadership; others give more weight to Jesus’ teaching on faith and prayer. The exact meaning of “out of season” is also debated. The secure starting point is the whole passage, not one puzzling verse taken alone.

That care also matters when we ask whether difficult biblical acts fit God’s goodness.

How to say it gently

You might say: “I can see why that sounds unfair. Mark even says it was not fig season. I do not think Jesus was punishing a tree for failing Him. The story surrounds His protest at the temple, so the tree seems to be a sign about worship that looks fruitful but is not. Could we read the whole chapter together?”

What not to say

Do not say the tree deserved punishment, invent facts about ancient fig harvests, or use the story to attack Jewish people. “It was only a tree” also misses why the scene troubles readers. Take the question seriously and show how Mark’s own narrative guides the answer.
