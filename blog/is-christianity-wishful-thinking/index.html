Is Christianity Wishful Thinking?

The comfort Christianity offers does not settle its truth; its historical and philosophical claims still need to be examined.

Published 28 October 2026 · Updated 28 October 2026

Practise in the app Back to blog
Answer in brief

Christianity can bring comfort, but comfort does not make a belief false or true. The decisive question is whether Christianity’s claims match reality, above all whether Jesus Christ rose from the dead. The New Testament invites that test: if Christ was not raised, Christian faith is futile, however consoling it feels.

The charge of wishful thinking deserves a fair hearing. People do sometimes embrace beliefs because those beliefs ease fear, grief or uncertainty. Christianity promises hope beyond death, so its appeal is understandable. But explaining why someone wants a belief to be true does not settle whether it is true.

Does comfort make Christianity false?

No. A belief can be comforting and false, comforting and true, or painfully true. Wanting a good diagnosis does not make it accurate; fearing a diagnosis does not make it wrong. The evidence must be assessed apart from how we feel about the result.

This is the gap in the objection. It moves from a claim about the cause of belief—our desire for meaning or survival—to a claim about the truth of belief. Psychologists can study what draws people to religion. That study, by itself, cannot establish whether God exists. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s discussion of Freud notes that even Freud distinguished an illusion shaped by desire from a belief proved false.

Is Christianity just a way to cope with death?

For some people, religion does help them face death. That admission is not a concession that Christianity is invented. The Christian claim is more specific: 1 Corinthians 15:14–19 says faith is empty if Jesus has not been raised. Paul does not defend hope merely because it helps people cope; he makes it depend on an event.

That matters because the resurrection is a historical claim open to questions about testimony, context and competing explanations. We explore how high the standard of evidence should be in our article on extraordinary claims. Evidence for the resurrection can be disputed, but the dispute cannot be resolved by calling hope a coping mechanism.

Would people invent the difficult parts of Christianity?

Some Christian teachings are far from flattering. Jesus calls people to repent, forgive enemies and take up their cross (Mark 8:34–35). He tells His followers to expect trouble (John 16:33). The apostles preached a crucified Messiah, a claim that could be shameful in their setting (1 Corinthians 1:22–25).

What should we do with psychological explanations of faith?

We should consider them seriously and apply the same standards to everyone. Christians can mistake strong feelings for evidence. Sceptics can also be influenced by desires for independence, certainty or freedom from religious expectations. Neither observation proves the other side wrong.

The stronger approach is to ask what each explanation can actually show. A psychological account might explain why a person believes. A historical argument asks what happened. A philosophical argument asks what follows from the evidence. These questions overlap, but none should be allowed to replace the others. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy discusses the limits and force of naturalistic explanations of religious experience.

What evidence would make a difference?

Start with the claims Christianity asks us to examine: the existence and teaching of Jesus, the accounts of His death and resurrection, and the credibility of the earliest witnesses. 2 Peter 1:16 rejects the idea that apostolic preaching was made-up myth. That statement is testimony to assess, not a substitute for assessment.

Historical sources do not give us a laboratory replay of the resurrection. Yet neither do they leave us with a mere feeling. Compare the testimony, its early setting and alternative explanations, including the questions raised in our discussion of hallucination claims. And remember that competing religions also make truth claims; sincerity and numbers alone cannot decide between them.

Can faith remain honest when doubts persist?

Yes. Biblical faith is trust in God, not a promise never to ask questions. The father who says, “I believe; help my unbelief” in Mark 9:24 gives voice to mixed faith and doubt. Christians should make room for careful enquiry, rather than treating anxiety or uncertainty as disloyalty.

In my view, Christianity is better served when we refuse two shortcuts: “it comforts me, so it is true” and “it comforts you, so it is false”. Both bypass the question that matters. If the Christian hope is real, it can bear honest examination; if it is false, we should want to know.

How to say it gently

“I agree that religion can help people cope with fear and loss. But something being comforting does not tell us whether it is true. Christianity makes a claim about Jesus rising from the dead. I would rather examine that claim with you than argue about what either of us secretly wants to believe.”

What not to say

Do not dismiss all psychology as hostile to faith. Do not say that suffering or persecution proves Christianity true. Do not imply that anyone who doubts is dishonest or merely wants to avoid God. Ask which claim is being tested, and keep the person’s fears and questions in view.

A thoughtful next step is to ask what evidence you would accept either for or against Christianity. That is more honest than trying to explain away another person’s motives.
