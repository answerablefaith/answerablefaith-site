How Can God Become Man Without Stopping Being God?

The eternal Son did not change from God into a man; He took our humanity to Himself while remaining everything He eternally is.

Published 12 August 2026 · Updated 12 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

The incarnation means the eternal Son took a real human nature; He did not stop being God. Jesus is one person who is truly God and truly man. His humanity is not an illusion, and His deity is not reduced. The Son remains what He eternally is while taking to Himself what He was not: our humanity.

How can God become man without stopping being God? The Christian answer is that the eternal Son did not change into a creature. He took a true human nature to Himself. John 1:1 and John 1:14 hold both truths together: the Word was God, and the Word became flesh. The incarnation is not God becoming less than God. It is the Son becoming what He was not before, truly human, while remaining what He eternally is.

Did God change into a human being?

No. The incarnation is not a transformation of deity into humanity. The Son does not surrender His divine nature, trade it for a human one or become a mixture of the two. He remains fully divine and assumes a complete human nature. That is why Colossians 2:9 can say that in Christ “the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily”.

This distinction matters because weak explanations usually fail in one of two directions. Either Jesus becomes a disguised God who only appears human, or He becomes a remarkable man whose deity has somehow been reduced. Scripture allows neither. Jesus can grow tired, suffer, learn and die according to His humanity while still being the eternal Son. That is why our article on why Jesus did not know the hour is not a separate problem. It is one of the places where His real humanity must be taken seriously.

Our view is that “addition, not subtraction” is one of the clearest ways to keep the doctrine straight. The Son adds humanity; He does not subtract deity. It is simple language, but it guards a deep truth.

What does Philippians mean when Jesus “emptied Himself”?

Philippians 2:5–8 says Christ “emptied himself”, but Paul immediately explains how: He took “the form of a servant” and was born in human likeness. The text does not say that Christ emptied Himself of deity. His humiliation is seen in what He took on and in the obedience He embraced, all the way to death on a cross.

That reading fits the rest of the New Testament. The same Jesus who is hungry, weary and mortal according to His humanity is confessed as “my Lord and my God” in John 20:28. His human weakness does not cancel His divine identity. The point of the incarnation is precisely that both are true of the one Christ.

We think this also corrects a common instinct. People sometimes imagine divine greatness as the inability to stoop. Scripture presents the opposite. The Son’s glory is not threatened by taking the servant’s place. His humility reveals the kind of God He is.

Why do Christians speak about two natures?

Because the Bible gives us one person who is fully divine and fully human. The language of two natures helps us say that without splitting Jesus into two persons or blending His deity and humanity into a third kind of being. The eternal Son is the acting person. He lives a genuinely human life because the humanity He assumed is real and complete.

The historic Definition of Chalcedon expressed this carefully by confessing one and the same Christ in two natures, without confusion or division. That language was not invented to make the doctrine sound clever. It was meant to stop Christians from solving one biblical difficulty by creating another.

This is where careful theology helps ordinary Bible reading. When Scripture speaks of Jesus’ hunger, growth, suffering or death, we do not need to deny those realities to protect His deity. When Scripture speaks of His eternal glory, divine authority and worthiness of worship, we do not need to weaken those claims to protect His humanity.

Why does the incarnation matter for salvation?

The incarnation is not an abstract puzzle added to Christianity. It is part of the gospel itself. Hebrews 2:14–17 says Christ shared our flesh and blood and was made like His brothers so that He could become a merciful and faithful high priest. He can truly represent us because He truly became one of us.

Yet our Saviour is not merely another human being standing beside us. The eternal Son Himself has come. 2 Corinthians 5:19 says that “in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself”. That is why the identity of Jesus matters so much. If He is not truly man, He does not stand in our place. If He is not truly God, the gospel has been reduced to a creature trying to rescue other creatures.

For us, this is one of the most compelling features of the Christian claim. God does not save from a safe distance. In the Son, He comes near enough to obey, suffer and die for His people, without ceasing for one moment to be the God who saves. The humility is real because the humanity is real. The rescue is secure because the Saviour is divine.

Does the incarnation mean the whole Trinity became human?

No. The person who became flesh is the Son. The Father did not become incarnate, and the Holy Spirit did not become incarnate. The persons are distinct, even though the one divine being is undivided. This matters because the New Testament repeatedly speaks of the Father sending the Son and of the Spirit resting on and empowering the incarnate Son in His mission.

That distinction also keeps our language from becoming careless. Saying “God became man” is true because the Son is fully God. But more precisely, the eternal Son became man. Precision here does not weaken the simplicity of the gospel. It helps us say exactly what Christians mean.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Christians do not believe God stopped being God and turned into a man. We believe the eternal Son remained fully divine while taking a real human nature. Jesus is truly God and truly man in one person. That is why He can genuinely share our weakness and suffering without His divine identity disappearing.”

What not to say

Do not say Jesus was fifty per cent God and fifty per cent man. Do not imply His humanity was a costume. And do not say the Father became the Son or that the whole Trinity became incarnate. The New Testament identifies the person who became flesh as the eternal Son.

A good next step is to read John 1:1–14, Philippians 2:5–8, Colossians 2:9 and Hebrews 2:14–17 together. Then compare this with the evidence for Jesus’ divine claims, how John 17:3 fits the deity of Christ, and what Christians mean by “Son of God”. Taken together, those passages show why the incarnation requires both full deity and full humanity, not a compromise between them.
