Christology · Incarnation and two natures

How Can God Become Man Without Stopping Being God?

The eternal Son did not change from God into a man; He took our humanity to Himself while remaining everything He eternally is.

Published 12 August 2026 · Updated 12 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

The incarnation means the eternal Son took a real human nature; He did not stop being God. Jesus is one person who is truly God and truly man. His humanity is not an illusion, and His deity is not reduced. The Son remains what He eternally is while taking to Himself what He was not: our humanity.

How can Christians say God became man without saying God stopped being God? The question matters because a careless answer can make the incarnation sound like God transformed into a creature. Christianity teaches something more precise. John 1:1 and 1:14 says the Word was God and that this same Word “became flesh”. The divine Son does not cease to be divine when He becomes human.

Did God change into a human being?

No. The incarnation is not subtraction from deity but the assumption of humanity. The Son remains what He is eternally while taking a complete human nature to Himself. This is why Colossians 2:9 can say that in Christ “the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily”.

That distinction protects both sides of the Christian confession. Jesus is not half God and half man. Nor is He a divine person merely wearing a human body. He possesses a genuinely human nature, including a human body and human mind, while remaining the eternal Son. Our article on why Jesus did not know the hour shows why His real humanity matters when we read passages describing human limitation.

What does Philippians mean when Jesus “emptied Himself”?

Philippians 2:5–8 does not say the Son emptied Himself of deity. Paul explains the phrase by saying Christ took “the form of a servant” and was born in human likeness. His humility is seen not in ceasing to be God but in the astonishing fact that the one who is divine stooped to serve, suffer and die.

This also fits the wider New Testament. The incarnate Son can hunger, thirst, grow tired and die according to His humanity, while Scripture still speaks of Him with divine identity and authority. That is why Christians can confess Jesus as “my Lord and my God” in John 20:28 without denying the very real humanity displayed throughout the Gospels.

Why do Christians speak about two natures?

The language of two natures helps us avoid two opposite errors. We must not divide Jesus into two persons, and we must not blend His deity and humanity into a third kind of thing. There is one Christ, the eternal Son, who is fully divine and fully human.

The Westminster Confession of Faith 8.2 describes the two complete natures as joined in one person “without conversion, composition, or confusion”. That Reformed wording stands in the stream of the historic Definition of Chalcedon, which likewise guarded the full deity and full humanity of Christ. The doctrine is careful because the biblical evidence requires care.

Why does this matter for salvation?

The incarnation is not an abstract puzzle added to Christianity. It is central to the gospel. Hebrews 2:14–17 says Christ shared our flesh and blood and was made like His brothers so that He could act as a merciful and faithful high priest. He truly stands with us because He truly became one of us.

At the same time, the one who saves us is not merely another sinner or creature. The eternal Son Himself has come. 2 Corinthians 5:19 says that “in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself”. In my view, this is where the doctrine becomes especially compelling: Christianity does not picture God shouting rescue instructions from a safe distance. In the Son, God enters our condition without ceasing to be the God who rescues us.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Christians don’t believe God stopped being God and turned into a man. We believe the eternal Son remained fully divine while taking a real human nature. Jesus is therefore truly God and truly man in one person. That is why He can genuinely share our weakness and suffering without His divine identity disappearing.”

What not to say

Don’t say Jesus was fifty per cent God and fifty per cent man. Don’t imply His humanity was a costume. And don’t explain the incarnation as though the Father became the Son or the whole Trinity became incarnate. The New Testament identifies the person who became flesh as the eternal Son.
