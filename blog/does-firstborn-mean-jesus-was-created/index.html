Does “Firstborn” Mean Jesus Was Created?

Colossians calls Jesus “the firstborn of all creation”, but the passage itself explains why that title places Him over creation rather than inside it.

Published 14 August 2026 · Updated 17 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Calling Jesus “the firstborn of all creation” does not mean He was created. In Scripture, “firstborn” can describe rank, inheritance and supremacy. Colossians immediately says all things were created through Christ and for Him. Jesus therefore stands over creation as its Lord and Creator, not within it as its first creature.

Does “firstborn” mean Jesus was created? At first glance, Colossians 1:15–17 can sound that way. Paul calls Christ “the firstborn of all creation”. But one word should not be isolated from the sentence that explains it. Verse 16 immediately says that all things were created through Christ and for Him. The context does not place Jesus among created things. It places creation beneath Him.

What does “firstborn” mean in the Bible?

“Firstborn” can refer to birth order, but Scripture also uses the word for status, inheritance and royal supremacy. In Psalm 89:20–27, God speaks of David and says, “I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth.” David was not the first child born in his family. The title describes the rank God gives him.

That matters because Paul uses the term in a paragraph about Christ’s supremacy. Jesus is called the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation, the one through whom all things were made, the one who is before all things and the one in whom all things hold together. The flow of thought moves upward, not downward.

Our view is that many arguments become weaker when they turn on a dictionary gloss and stop there. A word gets its force from the sentence and the sentence from the argument. In Colossians, “firstborn” is not left to float on its own. Paul surrounds it with Creator language.

Why does Colossians 1:16 matter so much?

Colossians 1:16 says that “all things” were created in, through and for Christ. Paul then widens the scope: things in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, including thrones, dominions, rulers and authorities. He is sweeping the created order into view.

If Christ were Himself part of that created order, Paul’s wording would become strained. The passage does not say that all other things were created through Him. It says all things were. Christ is presented as the agent and goal of creation.

The same Creator-creature distinction appears in John 1:1–3: the Word was with God, the Word was God, and all things were made through Him. John then sharpens the point by saying that apart from Him nothing came into being that has come into being. The Word is not placed inside the class of things made.

What does “He is before all things” add?

Paul continues in Colossians 1:17: “He is before all things, and in him all things hold together.” This is more than saying Jesus appeared earlier than something else. Christ exists prior to creation and continually sustains it.

Hebrews 1:1–3 gives the same shape. God made the world through the Son, and the Son upholds all things by the word of His power. Creation is not merely something Christ helped to begin. Its continued existence depends on Him.

We think this gives a useful way to explain the passage: Christ is not merely first in a queue. He is the reason the queue exists. That is stronger than saying “firstborn” just means “important”. Paul’s point is not vague prestige. It is supremacy grounded in who Christ is and what He does.

Does “firstborn” mean the same thing as “begotten”?

No. They are related ideas in some theological discussions, but they are not interchangeable words. Colossians 1:15 uses “firstborn” to describe Christ’s relation to creation. The historic Nicene Creed used the phrase “begotten, not made” to defend a different but connected point: the Son is eternally from the Father, yet He is not a creature.

That distinction matters because Christian theology does not say the Son came into existence at some point before the universe. The Son is eternal. John 17:5 speaks of the glory the Son had with the Father before the world existed. Colossians then describes this eternal Son as supreme over the world He made.

We would not make a creed do the work of the biblical text. The creed is useful because it shows how earlier Christians guarded the same Creator-creature distinction. But Colossians remains the centre of the argument.

Why this matters beyond one disputed word

This is not only about winning an argument over Colossians 1:15. It is about who saves us. The one who reconciles sinners to God is not the highest creature. He is the eternal Son who entered the creation He made. That gives the gospel its weight. The Saviour is not a messenger standing halfway between God and humanity. God the Son has come to redeem His people.

That is why this article belongs alongside how the eternal Son became truly human without ceasing to be God. The Creator did not turn into a creature or surrender His deity. He took a real human nature to Himself. It also connects with how John 17:3 fits the deity of Jesus and what Christians mean by “Son of God”.

For us, the broader lesson is worth keeping. Good theology does not protect doctrine by avoiding the difficult line. It stays with the line until the surrounding passage clarifies it. Colossians 1:15 looks like an objection only if verse 16 is left behind.

How to say it gently

You could say: “We understand why ‘firstborn’ can sound like ‘first created’. But the next verse says everything created was made through Jesus and for Jesus. Scripture also uses ‘firstborn’ for supreme rank, as with David in Psalm 89. So the context presents Christ as Lord over creation, not as the first member of it.”

What not to say

Do not mock someone for reading “firstborn” differently. Do not pretend the word can never refer to birth order. And do not build the whole case on one dictionary definition. Read the passage as an argument. Let “firstborn”, “all things were created through Him”, “He is before all things” and “in Him all things hold together” interpret one another.
