Does “Firstborn” Mean Jesus Was Created?

Colossians calls Jesus “the firstborn of all creation”, but the passage itself explains why that title places Him over creation rather than inside it.

Published 14 August 2026 · Updated 14 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Calling Jesus “the firstborn of all creation” does not mean He was created. In Scripture, “firstborn” can describe rank, inheritance and supremacy. Colossians immediately says all things were created through Christ and for Him. Jesus therefore stands over creation as its Lord and Creator, not within it as its first creature.

Does “firstborn” mean Jesus was created? At first glance, Colossians 1:15–17 can sound that way. Paul calls Christ “the firstborn of all creation”. But one word should not be isolated from the sentence that explains it. Verse 16 immediately says that all things were created through Christ and for Him. The context does not place Jesus among created things. It places creation beneath Him.

What does “firstborn” mean in the Bible?

“Firstborn” can refer to birth order, but Scripture also uses the word for status, inheritance and royal supremacy. In Psalm 89:20–27, God speaks of David and says, “I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth.” David was not the first child born in his family. The title describes the rank God gives him.

That matters because Paul uses the term in a paragraph about Christ’s supremacy. Jesus is called the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation, the one through whom all things were made, the one who is before all things and the one in whom all things hold together. The flow of thought moves upward, not downward.

Our view is that this is where many discussions go wrong. People sometimes argue over the dictionary meaning of one word while ignoring the argument of the paragraph. A better habit is to ask what job the word is doing in the sentence. Here, “firstborn” introduces Christ’s supremacy, and the next lines explain that supremacy.

Why does Colossians 1:16 rule out a created Christ?

Colossians 1:16 says that “all things” were created in, through and for Christ. Paul then expands the phrase: things in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, including thrones, dominions, rulers and authorities. He is deliberately sweeping the whole created order into view.

If Christ were Himself part of that created order, Paul’s wording would become very difficult. The passage does not say that all other things were created through Him. It says all things were. Christ is presented as the agent and goal of creation.

The same distinction appears in John 1:1–3: the Word was with God, the Word was God, and “all things were made through him”. John then adds that without Him nothing came into being that has come into being. That is strong Creator-creature language. The Word belongs on the Creator side of the line.

What does “He is before all things” add?

Paul continues in Colossians 1:17: “He is before all things, and in him all things hold together.” This is more than saying Jesus was born earlier than something else. Christ exists prior to the created order and continuously sustains it.

That fits the wider New Testament picture. Hebrews 1:1–3 says God made the world through the Son and that the Son upholds all things by the word of His power. Creation is not merely something Christ once helped begin. Its ongoing existence depends on Him.

We think that gives the passage a useful apologetic shape: Christ is not merely first in a queue. He is the reason the queue exists. “Firstborn” therefore speaks of supremacy in a context where Christ is already being described as Creator and sustainer.

Does “firstborn” ever mean inheritance and authority?

Yes. The biblical firstborn commonly held a place of inheritance and family authority. That background helps explain why the title can carry the idea of pre-eminence. The historical Nicene Creed later guarded the same biblical distinction by confessing the Son as begotten, not made. The point was not to replace Scripture with later terminology, but to rule out the claim that the eternal Son belongs to the class of created things.

We should still let Colossians carry the main weight. Historical theology can confirm how Christians have understood the issue, but Paul’s own explanation is already enough: all creation comes through Christ, exists for Christ and is held together in Christ.

Why this matters beyond one disputed word

This is not only about winning an argument over Colossians 1:15. It is about who saves us. The one who reconciles sinners to God is not the highest creature. He is the eternal Son who entered the creation He made. That is why the next part of Colossians can speak of reconciliation through Christ’s blood with such weight.

It also connects directly with how the eternal Son became truly human without ceasing to be God. The Creator did not turn into a creature or surrender His deity. He took a real human nature to Himself. Likewise, our article on John 17:3 and the deity of Jesus shows why difficult verses should be read within the whole biblical witness rather than made to cancel it.

For us, that is the broader lesson. Good theology does not protect doctrine by avoiding hard texts. It becomes stronger when the difficult verse is allowed to speak in context. Colossians 1:15 looks like an objection only when verse 16 is left behind.

How to say it gently

You could say: “We understand why ‘firstborn’ can sound like ‘first created’. But the next verse says everything created was made through Jesus and for Jesus. Scripture also uses ‘firstborn’ for supreme rank, as with David in Psalm 89. So the context presents Christ as Lord over creation, not as the first member of it.”

What not to say

Do not mock someone for reading “firstborn” differently. Do not pretend the word can never refer to birth order. And do not build the whole case on one dictionary definition. Read the passage as an argument. Let “firstborn”, “all things were created through Him”, “He is before all things” and “in Him all things hold together” interpret one another.

A good next step is to read Colossians 1:15–20 in one sitting, then compare it with John 1:1–3 and Hebrews 1:1–3.
