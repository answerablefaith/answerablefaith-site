Does “Firstborn” Mean Jesus Was Created?

Colossians calls Jesus “the firstborn of all creation”, but the next verse shows why that title exalts Him above creation rather than placing Him inside it.

Published 14 August 2026 · Updated 14 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Calling Jesus “the firstborn of all creation” doesn’t mean He was created. In Scripture, “firstborn” can describe rank, inheritance and supremacy. Colossians immediately says all things were created through Christ and for Him. Jesus therefore stands over creation as its Lord and Creator, not within it as its first creature.

This is a good question because the word “firstborn” can sound like a statement about who came into existence first. But Scripture itself gives us the context we need. We don’t have to avoid Colossians 1:15–17. Read as a whole, the passage gives Christians strong reason for confidence in Christ’s eternal divine identity.

What does “firstborn” mean in the Bible?

“Firstborn” can refer to birth order, but it can also mark status. In Psalm 89:27, God says He will make David “the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth”. David wasn’t the first son born in his family. The title points to the royal place God gives him.

That helps us read Paul carefully. Christ is “firstborn” because He has the supreme place over creation. The point is not that Jesus belongs to the created order. The point is His unmatched rank over it.

Why does Colossians 1:16 matter so much?

Paul doesn’t leave the title unexplained. He says that all things were created through Christ and for Christ, including things visible and invisible. Verse 17 then says He is “before all things”.

That makes a created-Christ reading very hard to sustain. If all created things owe their existence to the Son, the Son isn’t being presented as one member of that created class. Paul’s aim is to magnify Christ. This also fits the wider New Testament witness that all things came into being through the Word.

Christ’s supremacy is good news, not just a debate point

Reformed theology has long confessed the Son as eternally God, of one divine being with the Father and the Spirit. But this truth isn’t merely something to defend. The One who saves Christians is not a lesser creature trying to bring us to God. He is the eternal Son who entered His own creation for us. That gives weight to His promises and glory to His grace.

It also connects with how the eternal Son became truly human without ceasing to be God. The same Jesus who is supreme over creation genuinely took our humanity to redeem His people.

How to say it gently

You might say: “I understand why ‘firstborn’ can sound like ‘first created’. But can we read the next verse too? Paul says everything created was made through Jesus and for Jesus. The Bible also uses ‘firstborn’ for supreme rank, as it does of David in Psalm 89. So the context points to Jesus as Lord over creation, not the first part of it.”

What not to say

Don’t mock someone for reading “firstborn” differently. And don’t build the whole case on one dictionary definition. Let the passage do the work. Read the surrounding verses, compare Scripture with Scripture, and keep the conversation focused on who Paul says Jesus is.

When this question comes up, start with the text and stay there. Then connect it with the wider biblical picture. You can also explore how Jesus reveals His divine identity and how to read another common objection to Christ’s deity.
