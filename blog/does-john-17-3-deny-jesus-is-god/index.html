Does John 17:3 Deny That Jesus Is God?

Jesus calls the Father “the only true God”, but the same prayer and the whole Gospel of John include the Son within God’s eternal identity.

Published 29 July 2026 · Updated 29 July 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

John 17:3 does not deny that Jesus is God. Jesus names the Father as the only true God while also placing Himself beside the Father as the source of eternal life and claiming the glory He shared with Him before creation. John presents Father and Son as distinct persons within the one divine being.

John 17:3 should be taken seriously, not explained away. Jesus prays that people may know the Father, “the only true God”, and Jesus Christ whom He sent. The verse distinguishes the Son from the Father. It does not turn the Son into a creature.

What is Jesus contrasting in John 17:3?

Jesus is contrasting the true God with idols and false gods. He is not setting the Father against the Son. The sentence joins knowing the Father and knowing Jesus Christ as the content of eternal life. No prophet or angel is placed beside God in that way.

The distinction matters. The Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Father. Historic Christianity does not teach that Jesus is the Father wearing another mask. It teaches one God in three persons, which is why the question whether the Trinity is a contradiction must be answered by distinguishing one divine being from three divine persons.

Our view is that many objections to the Trinity begin by forcing a choice the text itself does not make: either the Father is God or the Son is God. John’s Gospel refuses that choice. It preserves real personal distinction while including the Son in the divine identity.

What does the rest of John 17 say about Jesus?

Two verses later, Jesus asks the Father to glorify Him “with the glory that I had with you before the world existed” in John 17:5. That is not the language of a created messenger. It points to the Son’s eternal fellowship and glory with the Father.

The wider Gospel says the same. John 1:1–3 says the Word was with God and was God, and that all things were made through Him. John 5:23 says all must honour the Son just as they honour the Father. Thomas calls the risen Jesus “My Lord and my God” in John 20:28, and Jesus accepts the confession.

These passages are not unrelated proof texts. They are part of the same Gospel’s sustained presentation of Jesus. Readers who want to see how this fits Christ’s own claims can continue with whether Jesus ever claimed divine authority and worship.

Does being sent make Jesus less than God?

No. In John, the Son’s being sent describes His saving mission and His relation to the Father, not a lesser nature. The sender and the sent one are distinct persons, but mission does not determine essence. A king’s son may be sent with the king’s authority without ceasing to share the royal identity; John’s claim is stronger still, because the Son eternally shares the Father’s glory.

John 10:30–38 holds these truths together. Jesus distinguishes Himself from the Father, yet says, “I and the Father are one”, and His opponents understand the claim as making Himself God. The question what “Son of God” means therefore cannot be reduced to Jesus being merely a religious teacher or created agent.

We think this gives a useful interpretive rule: statements about Christ’s obedience, mission or prayer should not be isolated from statements about His eternal glory, divine works and rightful honour. Sound Christology listens to the whole witness rather than allowing one category to silence the others.

How has historic Christianity understood this verse?

Historic Christian teaching has consistently confessed that the Father, Son and Holy Spirit share the one divine being while remaining personally distinct. The point is not to import a later idea into John, but to use careful language that protects the full pattern of the text: the Father is God, the Son is God, the Father is not the Son, and there is only one God.

The Nicene Creed’s doctrinal history matters here because the early church was not inventing a second God. It was finding language precise enough to affirm everything Scripture says about the Father and the Son without collapsing them into one person or separating them into two gods.

John 17:3 therefore affirms the Father as the only true God without excluding the Son from deity. The Father is God, the Son is God, and there is only one God. For readers wrestling with how these claims fit together logically, our article on the coherence of the Trinity develops that distinction further.

Why does Jesus call the Father “the only true God”?

Because He is praying to the Father and identifying Him over against every false god. Trinitarian theology does not weaken that confession. It insists that the Father truly is the only true God, while also recognising that John includes the Son within the identity, glory and works of that one God.

Our conclusion is that the strongest reading is neither to flatten the distinction between Father and Son nor to exaggerate it into a difference of nature. John 17 gives us communion, not competition: the Son addresses the Father, shares the Father’s glory and brings people into eternal life through knowing them both.

How to say it gently

You could say: “We agree that Jesus is speaking to the Father as the only true God. But the same prayer says Jesus shared the Father’s glory before creation. John distinguishes Father and Son while presenting both within the one divine identity. Could we read verses 3 and 5 together?”

What not to say

Don’t pretend the verse says something else. Don’t claim Jesus is secretly calling Himself the Father. And don’t answer one verse with a list of unrelated proof texts. Start with the prayer itself, then show how it fits John’s whole account. The same careful method is useful when considering how to answer difficult Bible questions without sounding defensive.

Read John 17 as one prayer. Let the distinction between Father and Son stand, and let the Son’s eternal glory stand too. Christian confidence is strongest when it does not evade a difficult verse, but shows how that verse belongs within Scripture’s larger and coherent testimony.
