Do Matthew and Luke Give Contradictory Genealogies of Jesus?

The two genealogies are genuinely different, but difference is not the same as contradiction; they can reflect distinct legal, family and theological purposes.

Published 21 August 2026 · Updated 21 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Matthew and Luke give different genealogies, but that does not prove a contradiction. Ancient genealogies could trace legal, royal or biological descent and could be arranged for different purposes. Matthew stresses Jesus’ royal Davidic line; Luke traces a broader line to Adam. The exact relationship between the two lists remains debated.

The genealogies of Jesus are a fair difficulty to raise. Matthew 1 and Luke 3 do not give the same sequence of names. Christians should not pretend otherwise. The question is whether the Gospels are making the same kind of claim in the same way, or whether they are tracing Jesus’ ancestry for different purposes.

What is Matthew trying to show?

Matthew opens by calling Jesus “the son of David, the son of Abraham”. His genealogy is tightly shaped around Israel’s story and royal hope. It moves through David and Solomon and presents Jesus as the legal heir to David’s throne. Matthew even arranges the genealogy in deliberate numerical groups, so it is already clear that his goal is more than giving a modern family-tree database.

Ancient genealogies could omit generations without claiming that no one existed between two named figures. The point was often ancestry, inheritance, covenant identity or royal succession. That makes Matthew’s structure selective, not automatically false.

What is Luke trying to show?

Luke places his genealogy after Jesus’ baptism and traces the line all the way back to Adam. That broader horizon fits Luke’s emphasis on Jesus as Saviour not only for Israel but for humanity. The line also reaches David through Nathan rather than Solomon, which is one reason the two lists diverge sharply after David.

One common Christian explanation is that Matthew gives Joseph’s legal royal line while Luke reflects another family line, often connected with Mary. Another possibility is that both preserve different legal and biological relationships within Joseph’s wider family. The approved Answerable source itself is careful here: these explanations are plausible, but the precise reconstruction is debated.

Does Joseph having two fathers prove a contradiction?

Matthew names Jacob as Joseph’s father, while Luke places Joseph in relation to Heli. That sounds contradictory if both writers are claiming the same biological relationship in exactly the same sense. But ancient genealogy could record legal descent, marriage relationships, adoption and levirate family structures as well as direct biological fatherhood.

That means there are historically plausible ways for Joseph to stand in two genealogical lines without having two biological fathers in the same sense. We should still be modest: the text does not spell out every family mechanism, so certainty about one harmonisation goes beyond the evidence.

What do both genealogies agree on?

Both Gospels identify Jesus with Davidic messianic hope. Matthew begins there directly, while Luke also connects Jesus to David and places Him within the whole human family. Romans 1:3 likewise says that God’s Son was descended from David according to the flesh.

That common claim matters. The genealogies are not two random ancestry lists accidentally placed beside each other. They are making theological and historical claims about who Jesus is: Israel’s promised King and the true man who comes for the nations.

How should Christians talk about the difficulty?

We should resist two bad instincts. One is to say, “There is obviously no problem,” which sounds evasive to anyone who has read the names closely. The other is to assume that difference itself proves contradiction. A better answer is: the lists are different; ancient genealogies could serve different legal and family purposes; several coherent explanations exist; and the exact reconstruction remains debated.

In my view, that is stronger apologetics than pretending we possess a certainty the text does not give us. Confidence in Scripture does not require overconfidence in every proposed harmonisation.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Matthew and Luke really do give different lists, so I would not dismiss the question. Ancient genealogies could trace legal, royal and family descent for different purposes. Matthew clearly stresses Jesus’ royal line through David, while Luke traces a broader line to Adam. There are plausible reconciliations, though some details remain debated.”

What not to say

Do not claim the Mary-line explanation is proven beyond dispute. Do not say ancient genealogies worked exactly like modern ancestry records. And do not tell someone they are dishonest for noticing the differences. The better case is careful: acknowledge the tension, explain the ancient context and distinguish an unresolved detail from a demonstrated contradiction.
