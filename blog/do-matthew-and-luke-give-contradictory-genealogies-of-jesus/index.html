Do Matthew and Luke Give Contradictory Genealogies of Jesus?

The genealogies are genuinely different, but difference is not the same as contradiction. Their structure, direction and purpose give good reasons to read them as complementary rather than careless.

Published 21 August 2026 · Updated 21 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Matthew and Luke give different genealogies, but that does not by itself prove a contradiction. Ancient genealogies could trace royal, legal or family descent and could be shaped for different purposes. Matthew stresses Jesus as David’s royal heir; Luke traces His place in humanity back to Adam. The exact relationship between the lists remains debated.

The genealogies of Jesus are a fair difficulty to raise. Matthew 1:1–17 and Luke 3:23–38 do not give the same sequence of names, and Christians should not pretend they do. The real question is narrower: are Matthew and Luke making incompatible claims, or are they tracing Jesus’ ancestry in different ways for different purposes?

What is Matthew trying to show?

Matthew tells us his aim almost immediately. Jesus is “the son of David, the son of Abraham”. His genealogy follows the royal line through David and Solomon and is arranged in three groups of fourteen. Matthew even draws attention to that arrangement in Matthew 1:17. That tells us the list is deliberately shaped rather than presented as a modern, exhaustive family database.

Ancient genealogies often served purposes such as inheritance, covenant identity and royal succession, and they could telescope generations. The Encyclopaedia Britannica’s overview of genealogy notes the long-standing legal and social functions of descent records across cultures. Matthew’s selectivity therefore does not amount to deception. His theological point is historical: Jesus stands in the line of Abraham and David and is presented as the promised King.

We think that distinction is important. A genealogy can be selective without being fictitious. The mistake is to assume that accuracy requires every ancient writer to use the conventions of a twenty-first-century ancestry website.

What is Luke trying to show?

Luke places his genealogy after Jesus’ baptism and works backwards, from Jesus all the way to Adam and finally to God. That wider horizon suits Luke’s presentation of Christ as the Saviour whose significance reaches beyond Israel to humanity as a whole. After David, Luke follows Nathan rather than Solomon, which is where the two lines diverge sharply.

One common explanation is that Matthew gives Joseph’s legal royal line while Luke preserves another family line, often associated with Mary. Another proposes different legal and biological relationships within Joseph’s extended family. Early Christian writers also tried to explain the divergence through family and levirate relationships. The historian Eusebius preserves one such early explanation in his Ecclesiastical History.

None of those reconstructions should be treated as certain simply because they are possible. The text does not give us every intermediate family detail. Our confidence should therefore rest on what the Gospels actually claim, not on pretending one proposed harmonisation has been proved beyond dispute.

Does Joseph having two fathers prove a contradiction?

Matthew names Jacob as Joseph’s father, while Luke places Joseph in relation to Heli. If both writers meant direct biological fatherhood in exactly the same sense, we would have a problem. But ancient descent language could include legal, dynastic and marital relationships as well as biological parentage.

That gives us plausible ways for Joseph to stand in two lines without imagining two biological fathers in the same sense. We should also notice Luke’s careful wording in Luke 3:23: Jesus was “the son (as was supposed) of Joseph”. Luke already signals that Jesus’ relationship to Joseph requires qualification because of the virgin birth.

For us, the key apologetic point is modest but strong: a contradiction must be demonstrated, not merely assumed from difference. If the social categories available to the writers allow more than one legitimate form of descent, then the apparent conflict is not enough to prove error.

What do both genealogies agree on?

Both Gospels identify Jesus with Davidic messianic hope. Matthew makes that explicit from the first verse. Luke also connects Jesus to David while locating Him within the whole human family. This agrees with Romans 1:3–4, where Paul says God’s Son was descended from David according to the flesh.

Both genealogies also sit inside accounts that affirm the virgin conception. Matthew says Jesus was born of Mary rather than generated by Joseph, and Luke says Jesus was the son of Joseph only “as was supposed”. So neither writer is casually confusing biological paternity. Both are using genealogical descent while simultaneously making clear that Jesus’ conception is unique.

This connects with the broader question of how the eternal Son truly became human. Christianity does not treat Jesus’ humanity as decorative. His real historical descent matters because the eternal Son genuinely entered our family line.

Why do the differences not weaken Scripture?

They should make us read more carefully, not less confidently. Scripture’s truthfulness does not require us to flatten every Gospel into the same literary shape. The writers can select, arrange and emphasise material differently while telling the truth.

We think this is a useful principle far beyond genealogies. Sometimes scepticism assumes that independent witnesses are trustworthy only if their wording and structure match. In ordinary life, that would often make us more suspicious, not less. Distinct emphasis can be exactly what we expect from genuine authors writing for different purposes.

That is also why we prefer careful harmonisation to forced harmonisation. A strong doctrine of Scripture does not need us to claim knowledge we do not possess. It gives us freedom to say both, “These accounts are true,” and, “We do not know every detail of how the family lines intersect.”

How should Christians talk about the difficulty?

We should resist two bad instincts. One is to say there is “obviously no problem”, which sounds evasive to anyone who has actually compared the names. The other is to assume that difference itself proves contradiction. A better answer is more precise: the lists are different; ancient genealogies could serve different royal, legal and family purposes; coherent explanations exist; and the precise reconstruction remains debated.

Our view is that this actually strengthens the conversation. Confidence in Scripture is not the same as confidence in every theory Christians have proposed about Scripture. When we separate the text from our reconstruction of the text, we can be both firmer and more honest.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Matthew and Luke really do give different lists, so we should not dismiss the question. But a difference becomes a contradiction only if both writers are making incompatible claims in the same sense. Ancient genealogies could trace royal, legal and family descent differently. Matthew clearly stresses Jesus’ royal line through David, while Luke traces His place in humanity back to Adam. Several reconciliations are plausible, though some details remain debated.”

What not to say

Do not claim the Mary-line explanation is proven beyond dispute. Do not say ancient genealogies worked exactly like modern ancestry records. And do not accuse someone of dishonesty simply for noticing the differences. The better case is careful: acknowledge the tension, explain the literary and historical context, and distinguish an unresolved detail from a demonstrated contradiction.

A good next step is to read Matthew 1:1–17 and Luke 3:23–38 side by side. Mark what each writer explicitly says, what each emphasises, and what neither explains. We think that exercise usually turns a supposed slam-dunk contradiction into a more interesting and much more manageable historical question.
