Can Christianity Ground Objective Moral Right and Wrong?

The moral question is not whether atheists can behave well, but why good and evil bind us even when belief, preference and power disagree.

Published 19 October 2026 · Updated 19 October 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Yes. Christianity grounds objective moral right and wrong in God’s good character and the equal dignity of people made in His image. This does not mean atheists cannot recognise or practise goodness. The debate is about what makes moral duties binding, not which group behaves better; thoughtful secular philosophers offer rival accounts.

Christianity can give an objective foundation for morality because goodness is rooted in who God is, not in changing tastes, laws or majority votes. That is a claim about the basis of morality. It is not a claim that only religious people know right from wrong.

What makes a moral wrong objectively wrong?

An objective moral wrong is not made right merely because a person approves of it or a society permits it. Torture, exploitation and abuse can be condemned even when the powerful applaud them. Christians locate that moral standard in the character of the God who is good, faithful and just.

Leviticus 19 joins God’s holiness to duties of honesty, fairness and love of neighbour. Micah 6:8 likewise calls for justice, mercy and humble obedience. These commands are not isolated preferences: they express the moral character of the One who gives them.

Does that mean atheists cannot be moral?

Not at all. People who reject belief in God can show great courage, compassion and integrity; Christians often fail at all three. Romans 2:14–16 describes people without the written law recognising moral demands, and Genesis 1:26–27 says all humans bear God’s image, not only believers.

We must separate three questions: Can someone act well? Yes. Can someone know something is wrong? Yes. What ultimately explains why it is wrong? That is the philosophical dispute. Our article on why justice matters to Christians explores the practical duties that follow.

Is something good only because God commands it?

This is the serious challenge often called the Euthyphro dilemma. If whatever God commands becomes good, morality sounds arbitrary. If God commands what is good because of a standard above Him, goodness appears independent of God.

The classical Christian response rejects both horns. Goodness belongs to God’s unchanging nature. His commands express that nature in the lives of His creatures. James 1:17 presents God as constant, while 1 John 4:7–10 links love to God’s very identity and His action in Christ. Christians need not imagine an arbitrary ruler inventing cruelty as a virtue.

This answer still requires philosophical defence: saying “God is good” cannot become a way of avoiding difficult biblical passages or the suffering of real people. The claim is that God is the measure of goodness, not that every human interpretation of a divine command is beyond question.

Could morality be objective without God?

Possibly, on some philosophical accounts. Secular moral realists argue that moral truths can be genuine features of reality without depending on a divine lawgiver. Others ground morality in reason, human flourishing or the interests of conscious beings. Such views deserve engagement, not dismissal.

The further question is whether those views can explain both the truth of moral claims and their authority over us when doing right is costly. A fact about suffering can tell us that harm occurs. Why every person has a binding duty to avoid unjust harm is a related but distinct question.

In my view, the stronger Christian argument is not “without God you have no morals”. It is that a personal, good Creator offers a coherent account of why persons matter, why love is fitting and why the powerful remain accountable even when they can escape punishment. This relates to the question of how competing worldviews should be assessed, rather than treating disagreement itself as a refutation.

What if Christians have used religion to justify evil?

Then Christians should admit it. Appeals to Scripture have been used to excuse oppression. That history does not become righteous because its defenders professed faith. Christian teaching supplies grounds to condemn their acts, but the existence of those teachings does not erase the harm done.

Our discussion of slavery and the Bible addresses one painful test case. The image of God also means that the oppressed person’s worth is not granted by a slaveholder, a government or a church. James 3:9–10 condemns treating people made in God’s likeness with contempt.

How to say it gently

“I’m not saying atheists are less moral than Christians. Many live with remarkable care for others. I’m asking what makes cruelty wrong even when everyone around us approves of it. Christianity answers by grounding goodness in God’s character and our shared dignity as people made in His image.”

What not to say

Do not say atheists have no morals, that secular philosophers have no serious reply, or that invoking God settles every ethical dispute. Listen carefully to the moral principle your conversation partner actually holds. It is better to identify the real disagreement than to win against a view no one has defended.
