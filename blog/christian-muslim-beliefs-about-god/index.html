Christian and Muslim Beliefs About God: What Is the Difference?

Christians and Muslims both speak of one Creator, but the deepest difference is who God has revealed Himself to be in Jesus Christ.

Published 17 August 2026 · Updated 17 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Christians and Muslims both affirm one Creator, but they do not mean the same thing by God. Christianity teaches that the one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and that the eternal Son became man in Jesus Christ. Islam rejects the Trinity, incarnation, divine Sonship and Christ’s saving death.

Christian and Muslim beliefs about God overlap at important points, but the overlap should not hide the central disagreement. Both faiths speak of one sovereign Creator who deserves worship. The decisive question is not simply whether God is one. It is who this one God is and how He has made Himself known. Christianity answers that question through the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, with Jesus Christ at the centre.

What do Christians and Muslims agree about God?

There is genuine common ground. Christians and Muslims reject idols and affirm that God created the world and is not part of it. Christians should be able to say that plainly. Deuteronomy 6:4 is foundational for Christian belief too: “The LORD our God, the LORD is one.” Christianity does not abandon monotheism when it confesses the Trinity.

We think this matters in real conversation. Starting with accurate agreement lowers the temperature and makes the disagreement clearer, not weaker. Respect is not the same as pretending two doctrines are identical. In fact, careful disagreement is usually more respectful than a vague claim that all religions are saying roughly the same thing.

Why is the Trinity the central difference?

Christianity teaches that the one God eternally exists as Father, Son and Holy Spirit. That is not three gods. It is one divine being, three distinct persons. The biblical pattern is already visible when Jesus commands baptism in the singular “name” of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit in Matthew 28:18–20. The persons are distinguished, yet gathered under the one divine name.

Islam rejects that account of God. This is where the disagreement becomes structural rather than merely verbal. Christianity does not begin with an abstract definition of divine oneness and then squeeze Jesus into it. It lets God’s self-revelation define what His oneness is like. Our article on whether the Trinity is a contradiction develops that distinction between one being and three persons.

Our view is that this is one of the most important habits in Christian theology: revelation must govern our categories. If God has revealed the Father, Son and Spirit within the identity of the one God, then our task is not to simplify that revelation until it fits an easier model. It is to understand it as carefully as we can.

Why does Jesus make the disagreement sharper?

The deepest divide appears in the identity of Jesus. Christianity confesses that the eternal Word was with God, was God, and “became flesh” in John 1:1–14. Jesus is not merely a prophet who directs attention away from Himself. He is the eternal Son who reveals the Father and shares the divine identity.

This is why John 20:28 matters so much. Thomas addresses the risen Jesus as “My Lord and my God”, and the Gospel presents that confession as the climax of its testimony to Christ. For a fuller treatment, see how Jesus reveals His divine identity and what Christians mean by “Son of God”.

Islam honours Jesus as Messiah and prophet but denies that He is God the Son. That means the disagreement over Jesus is not a side issue sitting beside a mostly shared doctrine of God. It changes the doctrine of God itself.

Why does the incarnation matter to the doctrine of God?

Christianity says the eternal Son did not stop being God when He became man. He took a true human nature while remaining fully divine. Colossians 2:9 says that in Christ “the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily”. This is why the incarnation is not the story of a creature becoming divine. It is the story of the eternal Son taking our humanity to Himself.

That distinction also protects Christian monotheism. The Son is not a second god added alongside the Father. He shares the one divine identity. Our article on how God can become man without ceasing to be God explains why Christ’s humanity does not reduce His deity.

We think this gives the Christian claim unusual force. God does not remain at a distance while sending a lesser being to rescue us. In the Son, the one who saves is the God who has been offended. The Judge Himself bears the cost of reconciliation.

How does Christianity describe salvation differently?

Christianity says salvation is an act of God’s grace from beginning to end. Human beings do not repair the breach by moral effort, religious performance or simply trying harder. Ephesians 2:1–10 describes sinners as spiritually dead and salvation as God’s gift, not something earned.

The centre of that saving work is Christ. 2 Corinthians 5:18–21 says that “in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself”. The gospel is not mainly advice about how to climb towards God. It is news that God has acted to reconcile sinners through His Son.

This is where doctrine and salvation meet. If Jesus were only a creature, His death would be the death of a creature. Christianity says something far greater: the eternal Son became man, obeyed in our place, bore judgement and rose again. Who Christ is gives His saving work its weight.

Do Christians and Muslims worship the same God?

This question needs care because people often use “same God” in different senses. Historically, both traditions claim to worship the one Creator of Abraham. But Christianity and Islam give sharply different answers about His identity, especially concerning the Trinity and Jesus Christ. Saying that should be neither evasive nor insulting.

Our suggestion is to move the conversation away from labels and towards revelation: “What has God shown us about Himself?” That question gets to the real issue. Christians believe the fullest revelation of God is found in the Son. Hebrews 1:1–3 says God has spoken by His Son, who is the radiance of His glory and the exact imprint of His nature.

The historic Nicene Creed reflects how early Christians guarded this biblical claim: the Son is not a creature alongside God but belongs within the divine identity. That history matters because it shows the doctrine was not invented to answer modern interfaith debates.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Christians and Muslims agree that there is one Creator who alone deserves worship. The main difference is that Christians believe this one God has revealed Himself as Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and that the eternal Son became man in Jesus. Could we look together at what the New Testament says about who Jesus is?”

What not to say

Do not say Muslims believe in three gods. They do not. Do not describe the Trinity as three gods either, because Christians do not believe that. And do not treat Muslim reverence for God as fake. State the common ground accurately, then explain why the identity of Jesus changes everything.

A good next step is to read John 1:1–18, John 20:28 and Hebrews 1:1–3 together and ask one focused question: who does the New Testament say Jesus is? We think that keeps the discussion where it belongs, on God’s self-revelation rather than on slogans about similarity or difference.
