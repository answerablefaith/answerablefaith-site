Christian and Muslim Beliefs About God: What Is the Difference?

Christians and Muslims both speak of one Creator, but the deepest difference is who God has revealed Himself to be in Jesus Christ.

Published 17 August 2026 · Updated 17 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Christians and Muslims both affirm one Creator, but they do not mean the same thing by God. Christianity teaches that the one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and that the eternal Son became man in Jesus Christ. Islam rejects the Trinity, incarnation, divine Sonship and Christ’s saving death.

The difference between Christian and Muslim beliefs about God is not simply that one religion believes in one God and the other does not. Both are monotheistic. The decisive question is what that one God is like and how He has made Himself known. Christianity answers that question through the triune God revealed in Jesus Christ.

What do Christians and Muslims agree about God?

There is real common ground. Christians and Muslims reject idols and affirm that God is the Creator, not part of creation. Christians can acknowledge that without pretending the two faiths teach the same doctrine of God. Deuteronomy 6:4 is central to Christian faith too: “The LORD our God, the LORD is one.”

That shared language is a useful starting point in conversation. It lets us speak fairly before naming the disagreement. Christian witness becomes weaker, not stronger, when we caricature Islam. Respect does not require us to blur real differences.

Why is the Trinity the central difference?

Christianity says God is one in being and three in person: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. This is not belief in three gods. The Westminster Confession of Faith describes the three persons as sharing one substance, power and eternity. Islam rejects this Trinitarian account of God.

For Christians, the Trinity is not an optional theory added to belief in one God. It comes from the biblical witness. The Father sends the Son, the Son speaks with the Father, and the Holy Spirit is given. Yet Scripture still confesses one God. If this distinction is the sticking point, our article on whether the Trinity is a contradiction explains the logic more fully.

Why does Jesus make the disagreement sharper?

The largest divide appears in the identity of Jesus. Christianity confesses that the eternal Word “became flesh” in John 1:1–14. Jesus is not merely a prophet who points away from Himself. He is the eternal Son who reveals the Father and shares the divine identity.

Islam honours Jesus as a prophet and Messiah, but denies that He is God the Son. That means the incarnation, crucifixion and atonement are not side issues. They sit at the centre of the disagreement. For more on the Christian claim, see what “Son of God” means and how Jesus reveals His divine identity.

How does Christianity describe salvation differently?

Christianity says God acts in Christ to reconcile sinners to Himself. 2 Corinthians 5:18–21 says that “in Christ God was reconciling the world to himself”. The gospel is therefore not mainly a message about human beings climbing towards God. It is the news that God comes to save.

That is why Christian teaching about God and Christian teaching about salvation cannot be separated. Who Jesus is determines what His death can accomplish. The Saviour is not a created intermediary. He is God the Son made man for us.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Christians and Muslims agree that there is one Creator who alone deserves worship. The main difference is that Christians believe this one God has revealed Himself as Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and that the eternal Son became man in Jesus. Could we look together at what Jesus says and does in the Gospels?”

What not to say

Do not say Muslims worship three gods, because Muslims do not. Do not describe the Trinity as three gods either, because Christians do not believe that. And do not treat Muslim reverence for God as fake. State the common ground accurately, then explain why the identity of Jesus changes everything.

A good next step is to read John 1:1–18 and ask one focused question: who does John say Jesus is? That keeps the discussion centred on revelation rather than slogans.
