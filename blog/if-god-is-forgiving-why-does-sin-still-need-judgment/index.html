If God Is Forgiving, Why Does Sin Still Need Judgment?

God’s forgiveness is not God pretending evil does not matter; Christian forgiveness is mercy given without denying justice.

Published 7 October 2026 · Updated 7 October 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

God forgives sinners without treating evil as though it never mattered. Scripture presents God as both merciful and just. The cross is where those truths meet: sin is judged, Christ willingly bears judgment for His people, and forgiveness is given without God denying His own righteousness.

If God can forgive, why not just forgive? That is a serious question, especially because the Old Testament sometimes says God forgave people directly. Christianity does not answer by saying God was unable to forgive until Jesus persuaded Him. The stronger answer is that divine forgiveness has always flowed from God’s mercy, while the cross reveals how that mercy can be given without making justice meaningless.

We think the question becomes clearer once we stop treating forgiveness and judgment as opposites. In Scripture, the same God who forgives is also the God who judges rightly. The cross does not force mercy into God’s character. It shows what God’s mercy looks like when His justice is taken with equal seriousness.

Does forgiveness mean pretending evil never happened?

No. Forgiveness and moral indifference are not the same thing. If a judge ignored violence, abuse or exploitation and called that mercy, we would not praise the judge as loving. We would say justice had failed.

Scripture describes God as the Judge who does what is right in Genesis 18:25. His holiness means evil is not finally waved away. His mercy means sinners are not left without hope.

That is why the Bible can say both that “the wages of sin is death” and that eternal life is God’s gift in Christ in Romans 6:23. Judgment and mercy are not rival moods in God.

We would go further: a forgiveness that required God to call evil unimportant would not be morally superior. It would make the victims of evil carry the cost of a justice that never comes. Biblical mercy is stronger than that because it does not need to deny the seriousness of the wrong.

But didn’t God forgive people before Jesus died?

Yes. The Old Testament repeatedly shows God forgiving repentant sinners. Numbers 14:19–20 is a clear example: Moses asks God to pardon Israel, and God says that He has pardoned.

That does not mean the cross was irrelevant. The New Testament presents Christ’s death as the basis on which God can be “just and the justifier” of the one who has faith in Jesus. Romans 3:21–26 explicitly looks backwards as well as forwards: God had passed over former sins, and the cross publicly demonstrates His righteousness.

So the Bible’s own answer is not that Old Testament forgiveness was fake or delayed. It is that God’s saving mercy across history rests on the atoning work of Christ, even before that work occurred in time.

This matters because the objection is strongest when it notices genuine prior forgiveness. We should concede that point, not avoid it. The cross is not needed because God lacked mercy before Calvary. It is the historical centre of the one saving plan through which that mercy is righteous, effective and finally secured.

Why was the cross needed if God is all-powerful?

Because omnipotence does not mean God can deny His own character. Christians do not believe goodness and justice are external rules imposed on God. Nor do we believe God invents morality arbitrarily. God is good, holy and just by nature.

The cross therefore does not satisfy an outside force. It reveals God acting consistently with who He is. In John 10:17–18, Jesus says He lays down His life willingly. The Son is not an unwilling third party. Father and Son act in one saving purpose.

This matters because the common objection often assumes a picture Christianity itself rejects: an angry Father punishing an unrelated innocent victim. The Christian claim is that God Himself, in the person of the Son, takes human nature and bears the cost of saving His people.

Our article on how God can become man without ceasing to be God explains why that incarnation matters. The one who bears judgment is not a random creature drafted into someone else’s sentence. He is the eternal Son who freely becomes man and acts in unity with the Father’s saving will.

What does it mean that Christ bears judgment?

The New Testament does more than say Jesus suffered as an inspiring example. It says His death deals with guilt. Isaiah 53:4–6 describes the servant bearing the iniquity of others, and 1 Peter 2:21–24 applies this pattern to Christ, saying He bore our sins in His body on the tree.

The point is substitution, but substitution within covenant union and divine purpose. Christ does not become personally sinful. He bears the judicial consequence due to His people so that they receive a righteousness they did not earn.

We think this also guards against a shallow version of “someone had to pay”. The Christian claim is not that punishment is a cosmic quantity that must land on any available target. It is that the representative Christ willingly stands for His people in the judgment their sin deserves.

Could God forgive without any cost at all?

Human forgiveness already shows why “costless forgiveness” is hard to imagine. When someone seriously wrongs you and you forgive, the wrong does not disappear. You absorb a real loss rather than retaliating or demanding full repayment.

The analogy is limited, but it exposes a hidden assumption. Forgiveness does not make consequences unreal. It means the offended party bears the cost of refusing vengeance. At the cross, Christianity says God does more than provide a moral illustration: Christ actually deals with guilt and judgment.

Our article on faith, works and justification explains why salvation cannot be earned after that. And why grace still produces good works shows that forgiveness does not make holiness optional.

Does the cross mean God is more just than merciful?

No. The cross shows that God’s justice and mercy are united, not balanced like competing forces. 1 John 4:9–10 describes Christ’s atoning work as an expression of God’s love.

We think this is the point many discussions miss. The cross is not a device that makes an unwilling God forgiving. It is the form God’s forgiveness takes because the God who loves sinners is the same God who refuses to call evil good.

That means mercy is not God temporarily suspending justice, and justice is not God overcoming His mercy. The cross reveals one holy love acting consistently with itself.

This also connects to why final judgment matters. If evil never finally mattered, victims would have no ultimate justice. Christianity says mercy is real because judgment is real, and rescue is offered in Christ.

Why does this matter beyond a theory of atonement?

Because the cross shapes how Christians think about both guilt and forgiveness. We cannot treat our own sin as trivial if forgiveness required the self-giving of Christ. But we also cannot treat repentant sinners as though their guilt were beyond grace if Christ has borne judgment fully.

That creates a moral seriousness without despair. Sin is worse than we like to admit, yet grace is greater than our ability to repair ourselves. We think that combination is one reason the Christian message resists both self-righteousness and hopelessness.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Christians do not believe God needed Jesus to persuade Him to forgive. God has always been merciful. The cross shows how God can forgive sinners without treating evil as meaningless: Christ willingly bears judgment, so mercy is given without justice being denied.”

What not to say

Do not say God was unable to forgive until blood changed His mind. Do not present the Father as cruel and the Son as loving. And do not tell someone that every Old Testament act of forgiveness was only pretend until Calvary.

Scripture presents one merciful and just God saving through one coherent plan. The Father sends, the Son willingly gives Himself, and sinners receive mercy that does not require God to deny His righteousness.
