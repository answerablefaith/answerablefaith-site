Christology · Atonement and Psalm 22

Why Did Jesus Cry “My God, Why Have You Forsaken Me?”

Jesus was quoting Psalm 22 as the righteous sufferer who bore sin and was vindicated by God.

Published 31 July 2026 · Updated 31 July 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Jesus cried, “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” because He was quoting Psalm 22 and bearing the judgment of sin as our substitute. The Trinity did not break apart. The Father still loved the Son, and the cross fulfilled God’s united saving plan.

Jesus’ words in Matthew 27:46 are not a denial of His deity or a collapse of the Trinity. They are the opening words of Psalm 22, a psalm that begins in anguish and ends in trust, deliverance and worldwide worship.

Was Jesus simply quoting Psalm 22?

He was quoting it, but not merely citing a text. Jesus was identifying Himself as the righteous sufferer described there. Psalm 22 speaks of mockery, divided garments, public shame and later vindication. Those themes meet at the cross.

The cry therefore carries both real agony and scriptural purpose. Jesus is not pretending to suffer. Nor is He losing faith. He prays Scripture from within the judgment He came to bear.

Did the Father stop loving the Son?

No. The Father, Son and Holy Spirit remain the one God. The divine being cannot be torn apart. The Westminster Confession teaches that Christ offered Himself through the eternal Spirit and fully satisfied divine justice. The cross is the work of the triune God, not a conflict inside God.

The Son truly experiences the horror of bearing sin according to His human nature and mediatorial office. Yet the Father does not cease to love Him. 2 Corinthians 5:19 says that God was in Christ reconciling the world to Himself.

What does this cry tell us about the cross?

It tells us the cross was not theatre. Christ entered the deepest darkness of judgment so sinners could be brought near to God. He was the sinless substitute, not an unwilling victim. As Hebrews 9:14 says, Christ offered Himself through the eternal Spirit.

Psalm 22 does not end in defeat. It moves towards vindication and praise among the nations. The resurrection confirms that the suffering Son was not abandoned to death.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Jesus was not losing faith or ceasing to be God. He was praying Psalm 22 as the righteous sufferer and bearing sin for us. The Father, Son and Spirit remained united in one saving purpose.”

What not to say

Do not say the Father hated the Son, that the Trinity broke apart, or that Jesus only felt abandoned because He misunderstood what was happening. Those claims distort both the psalm and the doctrine of God.

Read Psalm 22 from beginning to end, then return to the crucifixion accounts. The movement from anguish to vindication helps the cry make sense.
