Christology · Atonement and Psalm 22

Why Did Jesus Cry “My God, Why Have You Forsaken Me?”

Jesus was quoting Psalm 22 as the righteous sufferer who bore sin and was vindicated by God.

Published 31 July 2026 · Updated 31 July 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Jesus cried, “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” because He was quoting Psalm 22 and bearing the judgment of sin as our substitute. The Trinity did not break apart. The Father still loved the Son, and the cross fulfilled God’s united saving plan.

Jesus’ words in Matthew 27:46 are not a denial of His deity or a collapse of the Trinity. They are the opening words of Psalm 22, a psalm that begins in anguish and ends in trust, deliverance and worldwide worship. The cry is therefore both a real expression of suffering and a deliberate act of biblical interpretation from the cross itself.

Was Jesus simply quoting Psalm 22?

He was quoting it, but not merely citing a familiar text. Jesus was identifying Himself as the righteous sufferer described there. Psalm 22 speaks of mockery, public shame, pierced hands and feet, divided garments and later vindication. Those themes converge in the crucifixion narratives, which is why Matthew 27:35–46 should be read alongside the whole psalm rather than verse 1 in isolation.

The cry therefore carries both real agony and scriptural purpose. Jesus is not pretending to suffer, and He is not losing faith. He prays Scripture from within the judgment He came to bear. Our view is that this matters because it keeps us from choosing between two truths the text holds together: Christ suffers genuinely, and Christ interprets that suffering faithfully.

Did the Father stop loving the Son?

No. The Father, Son and Holy Spirit remain the one God. The divine being cannot be torn apart, and the eternal communion of Father, Son and Spirit does not cease at Calvary. The cross is the work of the triune God, not a conflict inside God. John 10:17–18 shows the Son willingly laying down His life, while Hebrews 9:14 says that He offered Himself through the eternal Spirit.

The Son truly experiences the horror of bearing sin according to His human nature and saving office. Yet the Father does not cease to love Him. 2 Corinthians 5:19–21 says that God was in Christ reconciling the world to Himself and that the sinless Christ was made sin for us. The language of forsakenness therefore describes the reality of judgment borne by the incarnate Son, not the destruction of God’s own life.

Was Jesus truly forsaken?

Christians should not weaken the force of the cry. Jesus did not merely feel lonely in the ordinary sense. He entered the covenant curse due to sinners and endured the darkness of divine judgment as the obedient substitute. Galatians 3:13 says that Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us.

At the same time, “forsaken” cannot mean that the Son ceased to be one with the Father or that the Trinity dissolved. The distinction between personal communion and penal judgment is essential. We believe the strongest explanation says neither too little nor too much: the suffering is real, the judgment is real, and the unity of God remains real.

What does this cry tell us about the cross?

It tells us the cross was not theatre. Christ entered the deepest darkness of judgment so sinners could be brought near to God. He was the sinless substitute, not an unwilling victim. Isaiah 53:4–6 describes the servant bearing the griefs, sorrows and iniquities of others, while the crucifixion shows that this burden was carried by Jesus Himself.

This also explains why the cry belongs with Christ’s victorious words rather than against them. In Luke 23:46, Jesus entrusts His spirit to the Father. In John 19:30, He declares, “It is finished.” The one who cries from the darkness is still the obedient Son completing the saving work given to Him.

Why does Psalm 22 end in hope?

Psalm 22 does not end in defeat. It moves from anguish to vindication, praise and the worship of the nations. The sufferer declares that God has not despised his affliction and that future generations will hear what God has done. That movement helps explain why the Gospel writers present the cross as both humiliation and the path to glory.

Our conclusion is that Jesus’ use of Psalm 22 is not a hidden code that cancels the pain of the cry. It is a biblical frame that reveals where the pain is going. The resurrection confirms that the suffering Son was not abandoned to death, which connects this question naturally with the historical reality of Jesus’ crucifixion and the Christian claim that suffering was followed by vindication.

How should Christians explain this without creating confusion?

Start with the text itself. Read Matthew 27:46, then read Psalm 22 from beginning to end. Explain that Jesus is both praying the psalm and fulfilling its pattern. Then distinguish carefully between bearing judgment and the Trinity breaking apart. This method is stronger than using slogans because it allows the biblical passages to set the terms.

It also helps to connect the cry with the wider identity of Christ. The same Jesus who suffers is the Son who shares divine honour, authority and glory. Readers exploring that larger question may find it helpful to consider whether Jesus claimed divine authority and worship, what “Son of God” means, and how John’s Gospel distinguishes the Father and Son without denying Christ’s deity.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Jesus was not losing faith or ceasing to be God. He was praying Psalm 22 as the righteous sufferer and truly bearing judgment for us. The Father, Son and Spirit remained united in one saving purpose, and the psalm’s movement from anguish to vindication helps us understand the cry.”

What not to say

Do not say the Father hated the Son, that the Trinity broke apart, or that Jesus only felt abandoned because He misunderstood what was happening. Those claims distort both the psalm and the doctrine of God. Do not reduce the cry to a quotation with no real suffering either. A careful answer must preserve both the depth of Christ’s anguish and the unity of God.

Read Psalm 22 from beginning to end, then return to the crucifixion accounts. The movement from anguish to vindication helps the cry make sense. Where the mystery remains difficult, the right response is not to flatten it but to speak with the same care encouraged in what to do when you do not know the answer: hold firmly to what Scripture says and avoid claims it does not require.
