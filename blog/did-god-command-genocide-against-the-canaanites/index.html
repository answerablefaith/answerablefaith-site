Did God Command Genocide Against the Canaanites?

The conquest texts are morally disturbing. A careful Christian answer must face the deaths of civilians, not explain them away.

Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Practise in the app Back to blog
Answer in brief

Deuteronomy and Joshua describe severe commands against Canaanite communities. Scripture presents the conquest as a particular act of divine judgment, not a licence for Christians or modern states to wage religious war. That does not erase the problem of civilian deaths or settle every moral objection. Christians should face the difficulty honestly and follow Jesus’ command to love enemies.

The strongest objection is not that war appears in the Old Testament. It is that Deuteronomy 20:16–18 and Joshua 6:20–21 speak of destroying whole communities, including women and children. If God is good, how could He command that? Christians should begin with the full force of that question, rather than rush to defend a slogan.

What do the Canaanite conquest passages actually say?

They describe both destruction and displacement. Deuteronomy orders Israel to destroy the inhabitants of certain cities. Joshua narrates devastating battles. Yet Exodus 23:27–33 speaks of driving peoples out, while Joshua 13:1 says much land remained to be possessed. The texts should be read together.

Some interpreters see stock victory language in descriptions of total destruction. That may explain why groups said to be wiped out appear again later. But it does not prove that no civilians died. A defence that makes the violence disappear fails to answer the text.

Was the judgment about ethnicity or wrongdoing?

The biblical explanation stresses judgment on practices, not the worth of an ethnic group. God tells Abraham that the Amorites’ wrongdoing has not yet reached its full measure (Genesis 15:16). Leviticus 18:24–28 warns that Israel too will lose the land if it adopts the same evil. Deuteronomy 9:4–6 denies that Israel receives the land because it is morally superior.

The story also includes Canaanites who are spared: Rahab and her household in Joshua 6:22–25, and the Gibeonites in Joshua 9. This makes an explanation based on racial hatred a poor reading of the narrative.

Yet saying that adults committed grave wrongs does not, by itself, explain why children should die. Children cannot simply be blamed for their community’s sins. That is the point a serious answer must not evade.

Does ancient battle language solve the problem?

Ancient Near Eastern kings sometimes used sweeping language to celebrate victory. Biblical scholars debate how far that pattern shapes Joshua, and how the accounts relate to what happened historically. A study in Themelios examines destruction and expulsion together. A scholarly article in Scandinavian Journal of the Old Testament argues that literary context does not remove the ethical force of the commands.

These are different questions: How should we understand the language? and Was the action just? Even if a battle report uses exaggeration, the moral question remains wherever non-combatants were killed. Nor can archaeology settle by itself whether a divine command was morally right.

Can Christians claim to know why every death was just?

No. Within Scripture’s own account, God is the judge of nations and the giver of life, so the conquest is presented as an exceptional judgment within Israel’s history. That is the theological claim Christians must assess. It is not proof that every detail of the conquest is easy to defend.

The death of children remains especially painful. Christians may trust God’s justice without pretending to know the fate of each person or the full reasons for each death. Saying “God ordered it” identifies the narrative’s claim; it does not finish the moral argument for someone who doubts the claim. Our article on the problem of evil considers that wider challenge.

Could a Christian use Joshua to justify violence today?

No. Israel’s conquest is not a standing command to the church. Jesus tells His disciples to love their enemies. He says His kingdom does not advance by His servants fighting to prevent His arrest (John 18:36). His mission to the nations is to make disciples, baptise and teach (Matthew 28:18–20).

Christians must never use ancient Israel’s warfare to authorise conquest, ethnic cleansing or attacks on religious opponents. That boundary also matters when discussing how Scripture has been misused to defend injustice and why Christian faith requires concern for justice.

Is “genocide” the right word for the conquest?

The term carries a modern meaning connected to an intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group. Scholars dispute how it applies to ancient texts whose stated rationale is divine judgment. But arguing over the label can distract from the human cost. Whether someone calls the passages genocide, forced displacement or holy war, the question of civilians still deserves a direct answer.

How to say it gently

Try this: “I agree that these passages are disturbing, especially where children are involved. The Bible presents the conquest as a unique judgment, not a rule for Christians. There are debates about the ancient war language, but I would not use them to pretend no one suffered. Could we examine the texts and the moral question separately?”

What not to say

Do not claim that every Canaanite child deserved death, or that anyone today may copy Joshua’s battles. Do not say ancient warfare was harmless or treat a word like “hyperbole” as a complete defence. A person asking about innocent lives deserves a serious answer.
