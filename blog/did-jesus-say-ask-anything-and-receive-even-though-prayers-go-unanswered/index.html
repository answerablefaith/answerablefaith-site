Did Jesus Say Ask Anything and Receive, Even Though Prayers Go Unanswered?

Jesus’ promises about prayer are generous, but they are not blank cheques detached from His name, His will or a disciple’s life in Him.

Published 28 August 2026 · Updated 28 August 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Jesus does promise that His disciples may ask and receive, but the promises are given to people who abide in Him, ask in His name and seek the Father’s will. Prayer is not control over God. Unanswered prayer therefore does not prove Jesus lied; it means His promises must be read in their full biblical context.

Some of Jesus’ words about prayer sound unlimited. John 14:13–14 says, “Whatever you ask in my name, this I will do”, and John 15:7 says, “ask whatever you wish”. Yet Christians also know the pain of praying earnestly and not receiving what they asked for. The right answer is not to weaken Jesus’ promise or pretend every request must be granted exactly as imagined. It is to ask what Jesus meant by prayer “in my name” and by disciples who “abide” in Him.

Does “ask anything” mean God must grant every request?

No. The words “anything” and “whatever” occur inside a relationship of discipleship. In John 15, Jesus speaks to people who abide in Him and in whom His words abide. That means the promise is not detached from His character, teaching and mission.

Other parts of Scripture make the same point. 1 John 5:14–15 says believers have confidence when they ask “according to his will”. James 4:3 warns that people can ask wrongly because their desires are disordered. The Bible therefore does not treat prayer as a technique for making God obey us.

What does it mean to ask “in Jesus’ name”?

It means more than saying “in Jesus’ name” at the end of a prayer. A name represents the person, authority and cause of the one whose name is used. To ask in Jesus’ name is to come to the Father through Christ and to ask as someone who belongs to Him.

That does not make our prayers flawless. Christians often ask for things they sincerely believe are good and later discover they misunderstood the situation. But it does mean Jesus’ promise cannot honestly be turned into: “Ask for anything at all, for any reason, and God is bound to give it.”

Then why do faithful prayers still seem unanswered?

Sometimes we simply do not know. Scripture gives several reasons prayers may not be answered as requested, but it does not give us permission to diagnose every painful case. A request may conflict with God’s wiser purpose. The timing may differ from ours. The answer may take a form we did not recognise. And sometimes God says no.

Jesus Himself gives us the pattern. In Gethsemane He asks that the cup pass from Him, yet submits: “not as I will, but as you will”. His prayer was neither faithless nor pointless. The Father’s will was accomplished through the cross rather than by removing it.

Does unanswered prayer mean Jesus’ promise failed?

No, because Jesus’ promise was never that prayer would replace the Father’s wisdom. It was that His disciples could pray with confidence as people united to Him. The same New Testament that records the promise also records Christians praying through suffering, delay and weakness.

It helps to distinguish a failed promise from a misunderstood promise. If someone reads “whatever you ask” while ignoring “in my name”, “abide in me” and “according to his will”, the problem is not that Jesus contradicted reality. The problem is that a relational promise has been turned into a mechanical guarantee.

How does this fit with God’s sovereignty?

It fits naturally with the Reformed view that God ordains both ends and means. Prayer is one of the means He genuinely uses. That is why our recent article on how an unchanging God answers prayer can say that prayer really matters without implying that God discovers new information or abandons His eternal purpose.

In my view, this also protects Christians pastorally. If we tell suffering people that every unanswered prayer proves weak faith, we burden them with something Scripture does not say. If we tell them prayer changes nothing, we empty biblical prayer of its force. The better answer is that God truly hears and acts, while remaining wiser than our requests.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Jesus really does give strong promises about prayer, but He gives them to disciples who abide in Him and ask in His name. The Bible also says we ask according to God’s will. So prayer is confident asking, not control over God. An unanswered prayer can be painful without proving Jesus made a false promise.”

What not to say

Do not tell someone their loved one was not healed because they lacked enough faith. Do not promise that God must grant any request if the right words are used. And do not dismiss unanswered prayer with a slogan. The biblical answer is both confident and humble: God invites us to ask, and God remains God.
