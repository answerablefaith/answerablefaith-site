Did Paul Contradict James on Faith and Works?

Paul rejects works as the basis of justification; James rejects a claim to faith that never produces obedience.

Published 2 October 2026 · Updated 2 October 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Paul and James do not contradict each other. Paul denies that works can earn or form the basis of our justification before God. James denies that a dead claim to faith is saving faith. We are justified through faith in Christ, and the faith that justifies produces obedience as its fruit.

At first glance, Romans 3:28 and James 2:24 appear to collide. Paul says a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law. James says a person is justified by works and not by faith alone. The tension is real, but a contradiction only follows if they are answering the same question in the same sense.

We think that is the key to reading them fairly. Paul is asking what gives a sinner a right standing before God. James is asking what kind of faith is real. Those questions belong together, but they are not interchangeable.

What problem is Paul addressing?

Paul is opposing the idea that sinners can establish a righteous standing before God through works. In Romans 3:21–28, justification is God’s gracious declaration received through faith in Jesus Christ, not something earned by obedience.

The same point appears in Galatians 2:15–21, where Paul opposes reliance on “works of the law”. His concern is the basis of acceptance before God. Christ saves; our works do not place God in our debt.

Paul goes even further in Romans 4:1–8. Abraham is counted righteous through faith, and David speaks of the blessedness of the person whose sins are forgiven apart from works. The ground of justification is therefore God’s grace in Christ, not human achievement.

This fits the wider argument of salvation by grace and the place of good works: obedience matters, but it is not the price paid for justification.

What problem is James addressing?

James is confronting a different error: someone who says he has faith while his life gives no evidence of living trust in God. James 2:14–26 repeatedly targets a barren profession. “Faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead.”

James is not describing a person trusting Christ and then asking whether enough extra merit has been accumulated. He is asking whether an empty claim deserves to be called saving faith at all. Even demons possess bare intellectual belief that God exists. That is not the faith by which sinners rest in Christ.

We would put it this way: James is not adding works to faith as a second instrument of justification. He is exposing counterfeit faith by showing what living faith does.

Why do Paul and James both use the word “justify”?

They use the same word in arguments with different emphases. Paul focuses on God declaring the ungodly righteous through faith. James focuses on faith being shown or vindicated as genuine through its works.

Abraham makes the distinction especially clear. Paul points to Genesis 15:6, where Abraham believes God and is counted righteous. James points to Abraham’s later obedience in offering Isaac in Genesis 22:1–18.

The chronology matters. Abraham is counted righteous in Genesis 15 before the offering of Isaac in Genesis 22. James even says Abraham’s faith was “completed” by his works and that the Scripture about his believing God was fulfilled. The later obedience did not replace the earlier faith. It brought its living character into view.

We think this is one of the strongest points in the whole discussion. The same Abraham can serve Paul’s argument about the basis of justification and James’s argument about the evidence of faith because Abraham first believes and later obeys.

Does faith alone make obedience optional?

No. Paul himself denies that conclusion. Ephesians 2:8–10 says salvation is by grace through faith, not from works, and then immediately says believers are created in Christ Jesus for good works. Galatians 5:6 speaks of “faith working through love”.

The biblical pattern is therefore precise: works are excluded as the ground of justification but required as the fruit of living faith. We are not accepted because our obedience is good enough. Yet the grace that justifies does not leave us unchanged.

This is why the question also connects naturally with whether baptism is necessary for salvation. Christian obedience belongs to discipleship without becoming an independent basis on which God justifies us.

Does James contradict justification by faith alone?

No, provided “faith alone” means living trust in Christ rather than bare assent. James explicitly attacks a kind of “faith” that consists only of words and facts. Paul likewise expects genuine faith to reshape life.

A helpful distinction is between ground and evidence. Christ and His righteousness are the ground of our acceptance before God. Faith receives Christ. Works then evidence that this faith is alive.

We prefer this language to saying simply “Paul means one kind of works and James another”. That can be partly true, but it does not go deep enough. The bigger issue is what role works play. Paul excludes them from the basis of justification. James insists on them as the necessary expression of genuine faith.

For a broader discussion of assurance and perseverance, see whether salvation can be lost.

What about James 2:24: “not by faith alone”?

Christians should not dodge the wording. James really does say a person is justified by works and not by faith alone. But the phrase “faith alone” in his argument refers to a barren claim that has no works. That is the “faith” he has been attacking since verse 14.

Paul is not defending that kind of faith. He is defending reliance on Christ apart from works as the basis of justification. James is denying that a lifeless profession counts as saving faith. Once both contexts are kept intact, the apparent contradiction becomes much less mysterious.

Our view is that difficult verses are best handled when we let them keep their force. We do not need to weaken James to protect Paul, or weaken Paul to protect James. The doctrines fit because living faith both rests in Christ and bears fruit.

Did the early church see this as a contradiction?

The tension between faith and works was discussed throughout Christian history, but the two texts were not preserved as though one had to be removed to save the other. They were received together as Scripture and interpreted in relation to the wider biblical witness.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s entry on justification is not about Christian justification and therefore is not useful here; what matters is the biblical and historical theological question, so Scripture should carry the main weight. For a historical window into how early Christian writers handled James, the surviving text of Augustine’s On Faith and Works shows that the relationship between faith and obedient life was being discussed centuries before modern denominational debates.

How to say it gently

You could say: “Paul and James are fighting different errors. Paul says our works cannot earn a right standing with God. James says a profession of faith that never changes how we live is dead. Christians are saved by grace through faith in Christ, and real faith then bears fruit in obedience.”

What not to say

Do not say James teaches salvation by moral achievement. Do not say Paul makes obedience optional. And do not solve the tension by pretending James 2:24 says something other than what it plainly says.

The better approach is to read each statement in the argument it serves. Paul protects the freeness of justification. James protects the reality of saving faith. Together they leave no room either for self-righteousness or for an empty profession that never bears fruit.
