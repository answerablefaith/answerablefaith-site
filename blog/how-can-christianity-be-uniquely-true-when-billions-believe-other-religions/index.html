How Can Christianity Be Uniquely True When Billions Believe Other Religions?

Religious diversity does not show that no religion can be true; it shows that competing claims about God, Jesus and salvation need to be tested rather than treated as interchangeable.

Published 4 September 2026 · Updated 4 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Sincere belief does not make a claim true. Religions make conflicting claims about God, Jesus, sin and salvation, so they cannot all be true in the same sense. Christianity claims uniqueness because of who Jesus is and what happened in His life, death and resurrection. The question is not who believes most sincerely, but which claims best fit reality.

Religious diversity can make Christian claims feel arrogant. Billions of people follow Islam, Hindu traditions, Buddhism, folk religions and other faiths, often with deep sincerity. Why should Christians think Christianity is true while so many others disagree? That is a fair question. But disagreement by itself cannot tell us which view is true. It tells us that the claims need to be examined.

Does sincerity make a religious belief true?

No. Sincerity matters morally, but it is not a truth test. Two people can hold contradictory beliefs with equal conviction. One religion may say Jesus is God incarnate; another may deny that. One may say He was crucified; another may deny it. Those claims cannot both describe the same historical reality in the same sense.

We think this is the first distinction that clears the fog. Respecting a person does not require treating every belief as equally true. In fact, taking another religion seriously means allowing it to say something definite enough to disagree with.

Do all religions teach basically the same thing?

No. There is overlap in some moral teaching, but the central claims differ sharply. Christianity teaches one Creator who is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, that the eternal Son became man, died for sinners and rose bodily. Islam rejects the Trinity and divine Sonship. Buddhism does not begin with the Christian doctrine of a personal Creator at all.

Those differences concern reality, not merely preference. Our article on the difference between Christian and Muslim beliefs about God shows why shared words such as “God” or “Jesus” can hide major doctrinal differences.

Why does Christianity claim to be uniquely true?

Not because Christians are wiser, morally better or more deserving. Christianity claims uniqueness because of Jesus. John 14:6 records Him saying, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life.” Acts 4:10–12 says salvation is found in no other name.

Those are exclusive claims, but exclusivity is not automatically arrogance. Every claim about reality excludes alternatives. Saying Jesus rose bodily rules out the claim that He remained dead. Saying God is personal rules out the claim that ultimate reality is impersonal. The question is whether Christianity has good reasons for what it claims.

Why is Jesus the right place to test Christianity?

Because Christianity is not merely a collection of moral principles. It is built around claims about a person in history. 1 Corinthians 15:12–19 makes the point unusually stark: if Christ has not been raised, Christian faith is futile. Christianity therefore places a historical claim near the centre of its truthfulness.

That gives the discussion a useful focus. Rather than comparing hundreds of religious practices at once, we can ask whether Jesus was crucified, whether the earliest resurrection testimony deserves serious historical consideration, and whether the New Testament presents Him merely as a teacher or as sharing the divine identity. See the historical case for Jesus’ crucifixion, whether the disciples could have hallucinated the risen Jesus, and how Jesus reveals His divine identity.

What about sincere people who never became Christian?

This question is usually personal before it is philosophical. Christians should not speak as though we can see the final state of every individual heart. Scripture assigns final judgment to God, who knows people completely and judges justly. Genesis 18:25 gives the basic confidence that the Judge of all the earth will do what is right.

At the same time, Christianity does not teach that sincerity itself saves. The gospel directs us to Christ, not to the intensity of our religious feelings. We would therefore avoid two opposite errors: pretending religious differences do not matter, and speaking as though Christians have earned a privileged position over everyone else. Salvation is grace, not a reward for belonging to the cleverest group.

What about spiritual experiences in other religions?

They should not be mocked or dismissed. People across religions report powerful experiences, answered prayers, visions, peace or a sense of presence. But experience alone cannot settle contradictory truth claims. Experiences need interpretation.

A Christian can acknowledge that another person had a profound experience without agreeing with every conclusion drawn from it. 1 John 4:1–3 tells believers not to accept every spiritual claim uncritically, but to test it by what it says about Christ.

Our view is that this is an important safeguard for Christians too. We should not criticise other religions for relying on experience and then treat our own feelings as self-authenticating. Christian claims should be tested by Scripture and by the public claims the faith itself makes about Jesus.

Is Christianity just saying “our book says we are right”?

No serious Christian case should stop there. Scripture has final authority for Christian belief, but Christianity also makes claims that touch public history. The crucifixion of Jesus, the early proclamation of His resurrection and the rise of worship centred on Him are historical questions as well as theological ones.

For example, the Encyclopaedia Britannica overview of Jesus reflects the ordinary historical recognition that Jesus was a first-century Jewish figure whose crucifixion belongs to the core historical account of His life. That does not prove Christianity by itself, but it shows that the Christian case is not built solely on private revelation detached from history.

We think Christianity is strongest when it refuses both shortcuts. “Everyone is basically saying the same thing” is too shallow. “Our religion is true because our religion says so” is too circular. The better route is to compare the actual claims and keep returning to the person of Jesus.

How to say it gently

You could say: “We do not think people in other religions are stupid or insincere. But sincerity cannot make contradictory claims equally true. Christianity stands or falls on Jesus, so we would rather compare the claims about Him and ask which view has the strongest reasons behind it.”

What not to say

Do not say, “Everyone else knows deep down they are wrong.” Do not caricature other religions. Do not imply Christians deserve truth because they are morally superior. And do not retreat to “just have faith” when someone is asking why Christianity should be believed rather than another religion.

A good next step is to read John 1:1–18 and 1 Corinthians 15:1–19. Ask what Christianity is actually claiming about Jesus, then test those claims rather than starting with the number of people who agree or disagree.
