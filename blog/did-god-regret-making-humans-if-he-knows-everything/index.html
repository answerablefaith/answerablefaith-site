Doctrine of God · Divine regret and foreknowledge

Did God Regret Making Humans If He Knows Everything?

Genesis 6 describes God’s real grief over human evil; it does not say He discovered a mistake or learned something He had not known.

Published 14 September 2026 · Updated 14 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

God’s regret in Genesis 6 does not mean He learned new information or realised creation was a mistake. It describes His real grief over human wickedness and His resolve to judge it. Scripture can therefore affirm both that God knows the future and that human sin genuinely grieves Him.

No. Genesis 6 is not portraying God as a fallible planner who expected one future and then discovered another. The passage says human evil grieved Him. The crucial distinction is between regret caused by mistaken hindsight and regret that expresses sorrow over a real evil. Only the first would conflict with divine omniscience.

What does Genesis 6 mean when it says God “regretted” making humanity?

Genesis 6:5–8 connects God’s regret directly with the depth of human wickedness and says He was “grieved to his heart”. The text is telling us something morally serious about God’s response to sin. He is not indifferent to violence, corruption or rebellion.

What it does not say is that God had been ignorant of what humans would do. Scripture elsewhere presents God as knowing the end from the beginning in Isaiah 46:9–10, while Psalm 139:1–6, 16 presents His knowledge as exhaustive rather than reactive. Our article on God’s foreknowledge and human choices explains why knowing an action does not make that action unreal or forced.

Does regret always mean someone made a mistake?

No. That assumption does most of the work in the objection. We often regret decisions because we lacked information: “If I had known, I would have chosen differently.” But sorrow over an outcome does not logically require prior ignorance.

We think this is the most important distinction in the passage. If every statement of divine sorrow is translated into “God wishes He had known better”, then a creaturely weakness has been inserted into the text. Genesis itself gives us no reason to do that. It presents perfect divine knowledge and real divine grief as compatible.

That matters beyond Genesis 6. A God who knows evil beforehand need not therefore approve it, delight in it or respond to it as though it were morally neutral. Foreknowledge does not flatten moral reality. God’s knowledge is exhaustive precisely while His judgement of evil remains perfectly holy.

What about 1 Samuel saying God regretted making Saul king?

The same pattern appears even more sharply in 1 Samuel 15. God says He regrets making Saul king, yet the same chapter says the Glory of Israel “will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man”. Those statements sit inside one narrative.

That should stop us reading “regret” in only one sense. Verse 29 denies that God is fickle or forced to revise His purposes like an unreliable human ruler. Verses 11 and 35 describe His genuine grief over Saul’s rebellion and His changed dealings with Saul. God’s character has not become unstable; Saul’s standing before the covenant King has changed.

This is related to how an unchanging God can act and respond in history. God’s dealings with creatures can change because creatures and circumstances change, without God becoming wiser, better, less holy or more informed than He was before.

Is this just dismissing difficult language as “anthropomorphic”?

It should never be. Calling biblical language accommodated or anthropomorphic cannot mean the words are empty. Scripture speaks truthfully about God in forms finite creatures can understand. Genesis 6 really tells us that human wickedness is grievous to God.

The better approach is to ask what the language affirms and what it does not require. It affirms real divine opposition to evil, real judgement and real grief. It does not require ignorance, surprise or a failed plan. That distinction lets the language keep its force instead of either literalising it into creaturely limitation or explaining it away.

Historic Christian theology has long held together divine omniscience, immutability and providence. A useful primary-source example is the discussion of divine knowledge in Thomas Aquinas, who argues that God knows contingent things without learning them as events unfold. We need not adopt every later formulation to see the point: perfect foreknowledge and genuine creaturely events are not contradictory ideas.

Does God’s grief make human choices more significant?

Yes. Divine foreknowledge does not turn history into theatre. Genesis 6 presents human wickedness as genuinely wicked and worthy of judgement. God’s prior knowledge does not remove the moral weight of what people do.

Genesis 6:8–18 then moves from judgement to grace towards Noah and God’s covenant purpose. The point is not that God lost control and improvised a rescue. The holy God judges evil while preserving His saving purpose.

We would go further: the passage is stronger if both truths are retained. If God is surprised, His sovereignty is weakened. If His grief is unreal, the moral seriousness of sin is weakened. Genesis gives us neither option. God knows, God judges, and God truly opposes the evil He has always known would occur.

That balance also helps with the problem of evil. Foreknown evil is still evil, divine grief is not ignorance, and providence does not mean God calls wickedness good.

How to say it gently

You could say: “When Genesis says God regretted making humanity, it does not mean He suddenly discovered that creation had gone wrong. The passage says He was grieved by human evil. Christians believe God knew what would happen, but knowing evil beforehand does not mean He approves of it or feels no real sorrow over it.”

What not to say

Do not say God was merely pretending to grieve. Do not solve the passage by denying God’s knowledge of future choices while ignoring texts that affirm His foreknowledge. And do not use “anthropomorphic” as a label that empties the passage of meaning. The point is real divine opposition to real human evil.
