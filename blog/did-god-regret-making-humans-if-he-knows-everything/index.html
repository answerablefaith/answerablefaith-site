Doctrine of God · Divine regret and foreknowledge

Did God Regret Making Humans If He Knows Everything?

Genesis 6 describes God’s real grief over human evil; it does not say He discovered a mistake or learned something He had not known.

Published 14 September 2026 · Updated 14 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

God’s regret in Genesis 6 does not mean He learned new information or realised creation was a mistake. It describes His real grief over human wickedness and His resolve to judge it. Scripture can therefore affirm both that God knows the future and that human sin genuinely grieves Him.

No. Genesis 6 is not portraying God as a fallible planner who expected one future and then discovered another. The passage says human evil grieved Him. The stronger reading distinguishes regret as mistaken hindsight from regret as sorrow over a real evil. Only the first would conflict with divine omniscience.

What does Genesis 6 mean when it says God “regretted” making humanity?

Genesis 6:5–8 connects God’s regret directly with the depth of human wickedness and says He was “grieved to his heart”. The text is therefore telling us something morally serious about God’s response to sin. He is not detached from the violence and corruption of His creatures.

What it does not say is that God had been ignorant of what humans would do. Scripture elsewhere presents God as knowing the end from the beginning (Isaiah 46:9–10). Our article on God’s foreknowledge and human choices explains why knowing an action does not make that action unreal or forced.

Does regret always mean someone made a mistake?

No. That hidden assumption drives much of the objection. We often use “regret” for mistaken decisions because our sorrow is tied to ignorance: we say, “If I had known, I would have chosen differently.” But sorrow over an outcome does not logically require prior ignorance.

Foreknowledge and grief are not opposites. God can eternally know that sin will occur while truly opposing and grieving that sin when it occurs in history. In my view, this is the key distinction: reading every statement of divine sorrow as “God wishes He had possessed better information” inserts a creaturely limitation into the text.

What about 1 Samuel saying God regretted making Saul king?

The same pattern appears in one of the Bible’s sharpest examples. In 1 Samuel 15, God says He regrets making Saul king, yet the chapter also says that the Glory of Israel “will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man”. These statements occur within the same narrative.

That makes a simplistic contradiction less plausible. Verse 29 denies that God reverses Himself like an unreliable human ruler. Verses 11 and 35 describe His grief and changed dealings with Saul because Saul has rebelled. God’s settled character and purpose have not become unstable; Saul’s relationship to the covenant King has changed.

This is related to, but distinct from, the question of how an unchanging God can act and respond in history. God’s actions towards creatures can differ as creatures differ without God becoming a different kind of being.

Is this just dismissing difficult language as “anthropomorphic”?

It should not be. Calling biblical language accommodated or anthropomorphic must never become a way to make uncomfortable texts meaningless. Genesis 6 really tells us that human evil is grievous to God. The language communicates truth about Him, even though it speaks in terms finite creatures can understand.

The Westminster Confession of Faith describes God as unchangeable and knowing all things while affirming His wise providence. Scripture gives the same balance: God is never surprised, yet His relations with creatures are real. His knowledge is perfect, His moral judgement is stable, and sin is genuinely hateful to Him.

Does God’s grief make human choices more significant?

Yes. Divine foreknowledge does not turn history into theatre. Genesis 6 presents human wickedness as genuinely wicked and worthy of judgement. God’s prior knowledge does not remove the moral weight of what people do.

Genesis 6:8–18 then moves from judgement to grace towards Noah and God’s covenant purpose. The point is not that God lost control. The holy God judges evil while preserving His saving purpose. This also helps us answer the problem of evil: foreknown evil is still evil, grief is real, and providence remains sovereign.

How to say it gently

You could say: “When Genesis says God regretted making humanity, it does not mean He suddenly learned that creation had gone wrong. The passage says He was grieved by human evil. Christians believe God knew what would happen, but knowing evil beforehand does not mean He approves of it or feels no real sorrow over it.”

What not to say

Do not say God was merely pretending to grieve. Do not solve the passage by saying He did not know what humans would choose unless you are prepared to reject the Bible’s wider teaching about divine foreknowledge. And do not use “anthropomorphic” as a label that empties the text of its force. The point is real divine opposition to real human evil.
