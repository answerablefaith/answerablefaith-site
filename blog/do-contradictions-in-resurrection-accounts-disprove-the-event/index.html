Do Contradictions in the Resurrection Accounts Disprove the Event?

The Gospel accounts differ in selected details, but difference is not the same as contradiction, and the central resurrection claim remains consistent across the sources.

Published 21 September 2026 · Updated 21 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

The resurrection accounts differ in which witnesses, angels and moments they emphasise, but differences are not contradictions unless the claims cannot both be true. The Gospels agree that Jesus died, was buried, the tomb was found empty by women, resurrection was announced, and the disciples encountered Jesus alive.

No. The differences in the resurrection narratives do not by themselves disprove the event. Some details need careful comparison, and Christians should not pretend every Gospel tells the morning in identical wording or sequence. The real question is whether the accounts make mutually exclusive claims or report different parts of the same event.

Do different lists of women create a contradiction?

No. Matthew names Mary Magdalene and “the other Mary”; Mark also names Salome; Luke names several women and says others were present; John focuses on Mary Magdalene. John’s account even has Mary say, “we do not know where they have laid him” in John 20:1–2, which leaves room for companions.

A writer can name one member of a group without claiming that person was alone. The stronger objection would require one Gospel to say only Mary was there while another says others were there. That direct denial is not present.

What about one angel in some accounts and two in others?

Again, mentioning one angel does not deny the presence of two. Matthew and Mark focus on the angelic messenger who speaks. Luke explicitly mentions two men in dazzling clothing in Luke 24:1–7. If two messengers are present and one is the spokesman, both descriptions can be accurate.

This is where some harmonisations overreach. Christians do not need to force every detail into one artificial script. We only need to ask whether the reports can reasonably refer to the same morning without contradiction.

Was it still dark or had the sun already risen?

The dawn language can describe different moments in the women’s journey. John says Mary came while it was still dark. Mark says the women arrived very early when the sun had risen. Matthew says “toward the dawn”, and Luke says “at early dawn”. Departure, travel and arrival take time.

It is therefore reasonable that the women set out before full light and reached the tomb around sunrise. The accounts all locate the discovery early on the first day of the week; they do not require the whole visit to occur at one instant.

Do the accounts disagree about what happened after the tomb?

They emphasise different appearances and movements. Matthew gives strong attention to Galilee. Luke gives strong attention to Jerusalem. John contains both Jerusalem appearances and a later Galilean appearance. Those emphases are not the same as saying Jesus appeared only in one place.

Likewise, Mark 16:8 says the women initially fled in fear and “said nothing to anyone”. That need not mean permanent silence. Matthew and Luke describe them later reporting to the disciples. Fear-driven silence in the immediate aftermath and later testimony can both be true.

The textually disputed longer ending of Mark should also be handled honestly. Modern Bibles normally mark Mark 16:1–8 and the later ending clearly. But the resurrection is already announced in verse 6: “He has risen; he is not here.” Christian belief in the resurrection does not depend on Mark 16:9–20.

What do all four Gospels actually agree on?

They agree on the central event: Jesus had been crucified and buried; women came to the tomb early on the first day of the week; the stone had been moved; the tomb was empty; heavenly messengers announced that Jesus had risen; and Jesus’ followers came to believe they had encountered Him alive.

Those common claims fit the wider New Testament witness. In 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, Paul passes on an earlier tradition that Christ died, was buried, was raised and appeared to witnesses. Our article on what evidence a resurrection claim should require looks at the broader evidential question.

Does variation make the Gospels less trustworthy?

Variation can raise questions, but it is not automatically evidence of fabrication. Independent accounts often differ in selection, order and emphasis. What matters is whether the differences destroy the shared claim or can reasonably coexist.

In my view, the better standard is neither “every detail must be identical” nor “any difference can be explained away”. We should test each alleged contradiction on its own terms. Some proposed harmonisations are stronger than others, but the commonly raised differences about women, angels, dawn and appearances do not overturn the core resurrection account.

This is also why the argument differs from our page on whether the disciples hallucinated Jesus. One asks whether the sources conflict; the other asks whether a psychological explanation can account for the testimony.

How to say it gently

You could say: “The resurrection accounts are not identical, and Christians should not pretend they are. But a difference becomes a contradiction only when two claims cannot both be true. The main differences about the women, angels and timing can coexist, while all four Gospels agree on the central claim that Jesus was found risen.”

What not to say

Do not claim the accounts match word for word. Do not treat every attempted harmonisation as certain. And do not dismiss a sceptic for noticing differences that really are there. The stronger response is to distinguish variation from contradiction and examine the specific details fairly.
