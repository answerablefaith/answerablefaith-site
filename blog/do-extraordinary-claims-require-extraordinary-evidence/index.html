Do Extraordinary Claims Require Extraordinary Evidence?

Christians should welcome a demanding standard of evidence, but the standard must test miracle claims rather than rule them out before the evidence is heard.

Published 9 September 2026 · Updated 9 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Extraordinary claims should face serious evidence, but “extraordinary evidence” is not a separate kind of evidence. The real question is whether the available evidence is strong enough to justify the claim compared with its alternatives. The resurrection should therefore be tested by the historical case, not dismissed in advance because miracles are unusual.

Yes, an extraordinary claim should carry a serious evidential burden. Christians should not ask sceptics to lower their standards for the resurrection. But the familiar slogan becomes unfair if “extraordinary” means that ordinary historical evidence can never count, however strong it becomes. The better question is whether the total evidence makes the resurrection a reasonable explanation of what happened.

What does “extraordinary evidence” actually mean?

Usually it means that the evidence should be proportionate to the claim. That is sensible. We need less evidence to believe someone missed a train than to believe someone rose from the dead.

But evidence does not change species when the claim becomes unusual. We still ask familiar questions: How early are the sources? Are the witnesses or traditions independent? What facts need explaining? Which explanation has the greatest scope and least strain? The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's discussion of miracles shows that philosophers have long debated exactly how testimony, prior probability and miracle claims should be weighed. A strict standard is legitimate; an impossible standard is not.

Does the slogan assume miracles cannot happen?

It can. If someone begins by assigning a resurrection effectively zero probability because dead people do not naturally rise, then no historical evidence will ever be enough. But that risks building the conclusion into the starting assumption.

Christianity agrees that dead people do not naturally rise. Resurrection is not proposed as an undiscovered biological process. The claim is that God acted. So background beliefs matter. If God does not exist, resurrection is not a serious explanation. If God exists, resurrection is unusual but not impossible. That is why the question of evidence cannot be separated completely from the wider question of God.

What positive evidence is there for Jesus' resurrection?

Christians should answer this directly rather than only attacking alternative theories. The case is cumulative. Jesus' death by Roman crucifixion is the historical starting point; our article on whether Jesus was really crucified explains why. Very early Christian tradition then proclaims that Jesus died, was buried, rose and appeared to named witnesses. 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 records that tradition and includes appearances to individuals and groups.

The New Testament also presents the resurrection as something offered for examination, not as a private feeling. Acts 1:3 speaks of Jesus presenting Himself alive “by many proofs”, while John 20:24–31 does not shame Thomas for wanting evidence; it presents his encounter with the risen Jesus as leading to belief.

Is eliminating naturalistic alternatives enough?

No. This is where some Christian arguments are weaker than they need to be. Showing problems with hallucination, theft or legend does not by itself prove resurrection. A bad alternative does not make another explanation true automatically.

The stronger case asks two questions together: what evidence positively supports the resurrection claim, and how well do rival explanations account for the same evidence? Our article on whether the disciples could have hallucinated Jesus therefore handles one rival explanation, but it should sit inside a positive cumulative case rather than replace one.

What would actually settle the disagreement?

Often the real dispute is not one isolated fact but the rule being used to interpret all the facts. A sceptic may regard any natural explanation as preferable because miracles are antecedently improbable. A Christian may be too quick to label every unexplained feature miraculous. Both approaches can overreach.

In my view, the better standard is: which explanation best fits the evidence once we state our background assumptions openly? That does not make the resurrection mathematically certain. It does make the discussion more honest. It also connects naturally with the wider question of why Christianity should be preferred to rival religious claims: claims should be compared, not protected from comparison.

How should Christians avoid overclaiming?

Do not pretend every disputed detail has the same historical certainty as Jesus' crucifixion. Do not say sceptics reject the resurrection only because they hate God. And do not claim that one weak naturalistic theory being false proves Christianity.

Christian confidence should rest on a cumulative case. 1 Peter 3:15–16 joins giving a reason for Christian hope with gentleness and respect. The manner of the argument matters because our aim is truth, not merely winning.

How to say it gently

You could say: “I agree that resurrection is an extraordinary claim and needs serious evidence. I just do not think ‘extraordinary’ should mean ‘impossible to establish’. The fair question is what historical evidence we actually have, what alternatives explain it, and whether resurrection becomes reasonable if God exists.”

What not to say

Do not say, “You just need faith.” Do not treat scepticism as irrational merely because someone asks for stronger evidence. And do not use the failure of one alternative explanation as though it automatically proves the resurrection. Give the positive case and let the competing explanations be compared.
