Do Extraordinary Claims Require Extraordinary Evidence?

Christians should welcome a demanding standard of evidence, but the standard must test miracle claims rather than rule them out before the evidence is heard.

Published 9 September 2026 · Updated 9 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Extraordinary claims should face serious evidence, but “extraordinary evidence” is not a separate kind of evidence. The fair question is whether the evidence is strong enough to justify the claim when compared with the alternatives. Jesus' resurrection should therefore be tested as a historical claim, not rejected in advance simply because miracles are unusual.

Christians do not need a lower evidential standard for the resurrection. If Jesus did not rise, Christianity is false in a central and public sense. Paul says exactly that in 1 Corinthians 15:12–20: if Christ has not been raised, faith is futile. That is a striking feature of the Christian claim. Scripture does not ask us to make peace with a resurrection that never happened.

What does “extraordinary evidence” actually mean?

If the phrase means that evidence should be proportionate to a claim, it is sensible. We need less evidence to believe someone missed a train than to believe someone rose from the dead. Our background knowledge makes one event ordinary and the other highly unusual.

But the evidence does not change species when the claim becomes unusual. We still ask how early the sources are, what they claim, whether independent lines of evidence converge, what facts need explaining and how rival explanations compare. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's discussion of miracles surveys the long-running philosophical debate about testimony, prior probability and miracle claims.

We think there is a useful test here. If “extraordinary evidence” means “evidence strong enough for an extraordinary conclusion”, that is rigorous. If it means “no historical evidence could ever establish a miracle”, it is no longer an evidential standard. It has become a metaphysical veto. The conclusion has been decided before the witnesses are heard.

Rarity is not the same as impossibility

Christianity agrees that dead people do not naturally rise. Resurrection is not an undiscovered biological process. The claim is that God raised Jesus. So the relevant background question is not merely, “Do corpses normally revive?” They do not. The question is also whether God exists and could act in His creation.

That does not allow Christians to put “God did it” into every unexplained gap. God's existence would make resurrection possible; it would not prove that Jesus rose. We still need reasons for believing that this particular event occurred. This distinction matters because probability cannot be discussed honestly while one side's whole view of reality is hidden inside the starting assumptions.

What positive evidence is there for the resurrection?

The case begins with Jesus' death. Our article on whether Jesus was really crucified explains why His execution under Roman authority is the historical starting point. The earliest Christian proclamation then says that He died, was buried, was raised and appeared to named witnesses. 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 records that tradition. For scholarly discussion of its early form and resurrection language, see James Ware's study in New Testament Studies.

The New Testament presents the resurrection as more than an inward feeling. Acts 1:3 says Jesus presented Himself alive “by many proofs”. Luke 24:36–43 stresses bodily continuity, including Jesus eating before the disciples. John 20:24–31 presents Thomas moving from doubt to confession in response to what he encounters.

Paul's testimony also matters because the resurrection message is not a late decorative addition to Christianity. Our article on whether Paul changed the message of Jesus explains why Paul's gospel belongs to the same apostolic proclamation centred on Christ's death and resurrection.

Why weak alternatives do not prove resurrection

Christians sometimes argue as though defeating hallucination, theft or legend automatically proves the resurrection. It does not. A weak rival explanation does not make another explanation true by default.

The better approach asks two questions at once: what evidence positively supports the resurrection, and how well do rival explanations account for that same evidence? Our article on whether the disciples could have hallucinated Jesus examines one alternative, but it belongs inside a cumulative case.

We think apologetics becomes stronger when we say exactly what an argument has achieved. Removing an objection is useful. Establishing a positive case is a different task. Blurring those two may sound confident for a moment, but precision is more persuasive over time.

The historical claim and the theological meaning

The resurrection is not an isolated wonder performed for spectacle. It is God's vindication of the crucified Messiah and part of His saving work. Romans 4:23–25 connects Jesus' resurrection with our justification, and Romans 8:11 joins Christ's resurrection to the future resurrection of His people. That is why the resurrection changes Christian hope in suffering: God has not merely given an argument about life after death; He has raised Christ as the first victory over death itself.

We should also distinguish evidence from saving faith. Christians can and should give reasons, examine testimony and answer objections. Yet no argument mechanically produces spiritual life. The same Scripture that gives public testimony to Christ also says that God shines in hearts to give “the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ” (2 Corinthians 4:6). That keeps apologetics in its proper place: evidence matters, reasoning matters, and neither makes us sovereign over the God whose acts we are investigating.

What would make the discussion fair?

Both Christians and sceptics should state their assumptions openly. A sceptic can make the prior probability of resurrection so low that no historical evidence could ever move it. A Christian can move too quickly from “unexplained” to “miracle”. Neither approach does justice to the evidence.

Our preferred standard is straightforward: which explanation best accounts for the evidence once the background assumptions are made visible? That does not produce mathematical certainty. Historical reasoning rarely does. It does stop the phrase “extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence” from doing more work than an argument.

The same fairness should govern comparison with other worldviews. Our article on whether Christianity can be uniquely true when billions believe other religions makes the same basic point: Christianity should not be protected from scrutiny, but it should be assessed according to what it actually claims.

How should Christians avoid overclaiming?

We should not pretend every disputed historical detail has the same certainty as Jesus' crucifixion. We should not say sceptics reject the resurrection only because they hate God. We should not claim that proving God's existence automatically proves the resurrection. And we should not treat one failed naturalistic theory as the whole case.

1 Peter 3:15–16 joins giving a reason for Christian hope with gentleness and respect. Those belong together. Confidence does not require exaggeration. In our view, a Christian argument sounds more authoritative when it is willing to say, “This point gets us this far, but not further.”

How to say it gently

You could say: “I agree that resurrection is an extraordinary claim and needs serious evidence. I just do not think ‘extraordinary’ should mean ‘impossible to establish’. The fair question is what historical evidence we have, how the alternatives explain it, and whether resurrection is a reasonable conclusion if God exists.”

What not to say

Do not say, “You just need faith.” Do not treat scepticism as irrational merely because someone asks for stronger evidence. Do not assume that proving God exists automatically proves the resurrection. And do not use the failure of one alternative explanation as though it settles the whole case.
