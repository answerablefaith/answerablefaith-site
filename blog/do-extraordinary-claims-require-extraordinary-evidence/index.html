Do Extraordinary Claims Require Extraordinary Evidence?

Christians should welcome a demanding standard of evidence, but the standard must test miracle claims rather than rule them out before the evidence is heard.

Published 9 September 2026 · Updated 9 September 2026

Practise in the appBack to blog
Answer in brief

Extraordinary claims should face serious evidence, but “extraordinary evidence” is not a separate kind of evidence. The real question is whether the available evidence is strong enough to justify the claim compared with its alternatives. The resurrection should therefore be tested by the historical case, not dismissed in advance because miracles are unusual.

Yes, an extraordinary claim should carry a serious evidential burden. Christians should not ask sceptics to lower their standards for the resurrection. But the slogan becomes unfair if “extraordinary” means that historical evidence can never count, however strong it becomes. The better question is whether the total evidence makes the resurrection a reasonable explanation of what happened.

What does “extraordinary evidence” actually mean?

Usually it means that the evidence should be proportionate to the claim. That is sensible. We need less evidence to believe someone missed a train than to believe someone rose from the dead. Unusual claims deserve careful scrutiny because our background knowledge makes them less expected.

But evidence does not change species when the claim becomes unusual. We still ask familiar questions: How early are the sources? What are they claiming? How many lines of evidence converge? What facts need explaining? Which explanation has the greatest explanatory scope with the least strain? The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's discussion of miracles surveys the long debate over testimony, prior probability and miracle claims. A strict standard is legitimate. A standard designed so no historical evidence could ever satisfy it is not really a test.

We think this distinction matters more than the slogan itself. “Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence” is useful if it means “do not be gullible”. It becomes circular if it means “a miracle would need evidence stronger than any historical evidence could possibly be”. At that point the miracle has been excluded before the evidence is weighed.

Does the slogan assume miracles cannot happen?

It can. If someone begins by assigning resurrection effectively zero probability because dead people do not naturally rise, then no amount of testimony or historical evidence can move the conclusion. But that risks building naturalism into the starting point.

Christianity agrees that dead people do not naturally rise. Resurrection is not proposed as an undiscovered biological process. The claim is that God acted. So background beliefs matter. If God does not exist, resurrection is not a serious explanation. If God exists, resurrection is extraordinary but not impossible.

This does not mean Christians get to use God as an escape hatch whenever something is unexplained. We still need evidence that points specifically toward resurrection. The existence of God would make resurrection possible; it would not by itself show that Jesus rose.

What positive evidence is there for Jesus' resurrection?

Christians should answer this directly rather than only attacking alternative theories. The case is cumulative. Jesus' death by Roman crucifixion is the historical starting point; our article on whether Jesus was really crucified explains why. Very early Christian tradition then proclaims that Jesus died, was buried, rose and appeared to named witnesses. 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 records that tradition and includes appearances to individuals and groups.

The New Testament also presents the resurrection as something offered for examination, not merely as an inward feeling. Acts 1:3 says Jesus presented Himself alive “by many proofs”. Luke 24:36–43 deliberately stresses bodily continuity, including Jesus eating before the disciples. John 20:24–31 presents Thomas moving from doubt to belief in response to what he encounters.

Paul also ties Christianity to a public claim that could fail. In 1 Corinthians 15:12–20, he says that if Christ has not been raised, Christian faith is futile. That is striking. The apostolic case is not “believe regardless of what happened”. It rests on an event claimed to have occurred in history.

Is eliminating naturalistic alternatives enough?

No. This is where some Christian arguments are weaker than they need to be. Showing problems with hallucination, theft or legend does not by itself prove resurrection. A bad alternative does not make another explanation true automatically.

The stronger case asks two questions together: what evidence positively supports the resurrection claim, and how well do rival explanations account for the same evidence? Our article on whether the disciples could have hallucinated Jesus therefore handles one rival explanation, but it should sit inside a positive cumulative case rather than replace one.

We would go further: Christians should be willing to say when an argument only removes an objection instead of establishing the resurrection itself. That makes the case more credible, not less. It shows we are distinguishing between evidence for a claim and weaknesses in competing claims.

What would actually settle the disagreement?

Often the deepest dispute is not one isolated fact but the rule being used to interpret all the facts. A sceptic may regard any natural explanation as preferable because miracles are antecedently improbable. A Christian may be too quick to label every unexplained feature miraculous. Both approaches can overreach.

Our preferred standard is simple: which explanation best fits the evidence once the relevant background assumptions are stated openly? That does not make the resurrection mathematically certain. Historical reasoning rarely works that way. It does make the discussion more honest because the evidential standard itself becomes visible.

This also connects with why Christianity should be compared with rival religious claims. Christianity should not be protected from scrutiny. But neither should it be judged by a rule that would dismiss any miracle claim before evidence could matter.

What does Christian theology add to the historical question?

The resurrection is not an isolated wonder performed for spectacle. In Christian theology it is God's vindication of the crucified Messiah and the beginning of new creation. Romans 4:23–25 connects Jesus' resurrection with justification, while Romans 8:11 links the Spirit who raised Jesus with the future resurrection of believers.

That theological meaning should not be used to bypass the historical question. It comes after it. If Jesus was not raised, the meaning collapses. If He was raised, then we are no longer asking only whether something unusual happened. We are asking what God was declaring through it.

How should Christians avoid overclaiming?

Do not pretend every disputed detail has the same historical certainty as Jesus' crucifixion. Do not say sceptics reject the resurrection only because they hate God. And do not claim that one weak naturalistic theory being false proves Christianity.

Christian confidence should rest on a cumulative case. 1 Peter 3:15–16 joins giving a reason for Christian hope with gentleness and respect. We think that combination is important. Intellectual confidence becomes stronger when it is precise enough to admit what an argument does and does not prove.

How to say it gently

You could say: “I agree that resurrection is an extraordinary claim and needs serious evidence. I just do not think ‘extraordinary’ should mean ‘impossible to establish’. The fair question is what historical evidence we actually have, what alternatives explain it, and whether resurrection becomes reasonable if God exists.”

What not to say

Do not say, “You just need faith.” Do not treat scepticism as irrational merely because someone asks for stronger evidence. Do not assume that proving God exists automatically proves the resurrection. And do not use the failure of one alternative explanation as though it settles the whole case.
